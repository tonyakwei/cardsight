# Story Taste Guide

Working draft of Anthony's story taste as inferred from the QRians project, its revision history, and the playtest fixes.

This is not a theory document. It is a practical guide for writing, revising, and reviewing future CardSight stories. If Anthony states a preference that conflicts with anything below, Anthony's explicit preference wins.

## What This Is For

Use this guide when:
- drafting new story briefs
- revising mission blurbs
- rewriting reveals and consequences
- reviewing whether a puzzle feels like it belongs in the story
- asking agents to propose fixes without flattening the voice

## Core Taste

### 1. Institutional dread over creature horror

The strongest material is not "there is a monster." It is "a civilization built systems around a truth it could not survive cleanly."

Prefer:
- records
- procedures
- warnings
- logistics
- architecture used as evidence

Prefer less:
- jump-scare menace
- vague supernatural phrasing
- ornamental spookiness with no social or institutional meaning

### 2. Revelations should recontextualize, not merely inform

The best story beats make prior evidence read differently.

Prefer:
- "the flood was not a hazard, it was part of the lock"
- "the pantry was not ordinary storage, it was anti-drift medicine"
- "the wall was not decoration, it was a civilization talking itself through corruption"

Prefer less:
- isolated lore facts
- proper nouns with no interpretive consequence
- exposition that does not change how earlier scenes are understood

### 3. The world should feel physically specific

The prose works best when it gives a player something they could point at across a room.

Prefer:
- ledges
- grooves
- damp paper
- corroded chains
- smooth hand-worn hollows
- objects with weight, residue, color, or damage

Prefer less:
- abstract scene setting
- generalized "ancient mystery" language
- technical description that does not create an image

### 4. House voice should do real work

The three-house structure is not cosmetic. Each house should interpret the same world differently.

Prefer:
- Drake as blunt, tactical, transactional, dry
- Jones as earnest, scholarly, lecture-shaped, mildly comic
- Croft as stylish, physical, confident, a little performative

Prefer less:
- neutral prose that any house could have written
- repeating the same joke structure across houses
- flattening the houses into generic puzzle teams

### 5. Puzzles should feel like acts of investigation

The puzzle should feel like a plausible way of extracting meaning from the world.

Prefer:
- decoding, sequencing, reconstruction, comparison, interpretation
- solving that resembles archaeology, translation, engineering, or field reasoning

Prefer less:
- arbitrary-feeling party-game mechanics
- pure trivia with no supporting structure
- a puzzle whose skin is story but whose logic is unrelated to the story beat

### 6. The reveal should carry emotional force

A correct answer is not enough. The payoff needs a point of view.

Prefer:
- a reveal that says what this means for the house's theory
- a reveal that sharpens the moral or institutional picture
- a line strong enough that players may quote it back later

Prefer less:
- "you solved it and found X"
- lore hidden in procedural cleanup
- mechanically correct but emotionally inert reveal text

### 7. Human residue matters

The strongest moments are often small and personal inside a large system.

Prefer:
- final messages
- diaries
- testimony
- evidence of compromise, denial, exhaustion, or moral bookkeeping

Prefer less:
- purely cosmic framing with no individual human cost
- abstract disaster without named or implied people inside it

### 8. Moral horror lands best when written plainly

The grimmest material works because it is stated with bureaucratic or factual calm, not because the prose strains to be dark.

Prefer:
- ledger language
- official orders
- clipped testimony
- matter-of-fact brutality

Prefer less:
- melodramatic condemnation in the authorial voice
- purple prose announcing that something is evil

### 9. Diegesis matters

Even small UI or wording choices matter if they break the frame.

Prefer:
- "write" over "enter" when the player is imagining inscription work
- mission prompts that sound like tasks in the world
- category names that are evocative but not spoilerish

Prefer less:
- product-y UI language
- puzzle prompts that sound like form fields
- terminology that exposes the system too nakedly

### 10. Endings should be argued from evidence

The finale should feel like a room of people trying to decide what the discovered history demands, not just selecting an alignment.

Prefer:
- houses with evidence-rich but incomplete positions
- tradeoffs that feel painful
- clauses and decisions that reflect what was learned

Prefer less:
- endings where one side is obviously right
- philosophy unsupported by playable evidence
- choices that differ only cosmetically

## Anti-Patterns

Flag these aggressively:

- Generic "mystery temple" prose that could belong to any setting.
- A lore reveal that arrives as a noun before players have evidence for it.
- A puzzle that feels like trivia instead of investigation.
- A reveal that explains the answer but not its meaning.
- A mission whose mechanic and narrative payload feel stapled together.
- House voice that drifts into neutral competent-adventurer prose.
- A clue name that is so explicit it ruins the fiction, or so vague it cannot be traded for.
- Moral stakes explained in the narrator's voice instead of discovered in records or consequences.
- Finale positions that are interesting philosophically but unsupported materially.

## Tradeoff Rules

When the writing has to choose, prefer:

1. Narrative clarity over puzzle cleverness.
2. One strong revelation over three medium ones.
3. Concrete imagery over abstract atmosphere.
4. Distinct house voice over globally consistent neutral prose.
5. Evidence that changes interpretation over lore terminology.
6. Plainspoken moral horror over ornate darkness.
7. Diegetic immersion over slick UI phrasing.
8. Evidence-balanced deliberation over a cleaner but predetermined ending.

## Annotated Examples

These are here so future agents can see the taste in action.

### Keep: Drake's Act 1 intro

Why it works:
- It is efficient and characterful.
- The flood blame is folded into voice, not explained externally.
- "Got a job to do" gives Drake a sharp operational frame.

What to preserve:
- blunt tempo
- mercenary pragmatism
- contempt that stays funny

### Keep: Jones's lecture-shaped humor

Why it works:
- "Dr. Jones always said..." and numbered lectures make the house identity specific.
- The comedy reveals devotion and inexperience at the same time.

What to preserve:
- academic earnestness
- overpreparedness
- lightly comic self-importance

### Keep: Croft's stylish bravado

Why it works:
- The house feels physical, image-conscious, and loyal to Lara without becoming parody.
- "Tie your hair back. Let's go." is compact and memorable.

What to preserve:
- kinetic confidence
- glamour under pressure
- confidence with a trace of fear

### Keep: "We who built this will never let them leave."

Why it works:
- It converts architecture into intention.
- It reclassifies the temple as a prison, not a monument.
- The line is simple enough to land immediately.

Lesson:
- When a mission contains a line this strong, the reveal text must spotlight it.

### Keep: the Drag Marks inscription

Why it works:
- "Tell my child I loved them still" gives a personal wound inside the larger system.
- It prevents the setting from becoming only institutional abstraction.

Lesson:
- Preserve small human residue amid large-scale lore.

### Keep: Vesh's record framed as ledger language

Why it works:
- The horror is in how cleanly it is recorded.
- The prose does not overperform disgust; the bureaucracy damns itself.

Lesson:
- Moral horror is stronger when the text trusts the reader.

### Keep: the Pantry reveal as anti-drift medicine

Why it works:
- Ordinary domestic items become evidence of civilizational adaptation.
- "They medicated themselves with breakfast" is a strong interpretive turn.

Lesson:
- Favor reveals that make mundane evidence uncanny in retrospect.

### Keep: the Sighting Wall reveal

Why it works:
- It turns sky observations into a question about geography and disease.
- It widens the scale of the story without becoming abstract.

Lesson:
- Good reveals move from object-level detail to worldview-level consequence.

### Keep: the Wall of Repetitions as narrative spine

Why it works:
- The lines are plain, memorable, and cumulative.
- They show realization happening inside the civilization, not in retrospective summary.

Lesson:
- The core mythology should appear in playable text, not only in lore docs or host explanation.

### Keep: the "write" register

Why it works:
- It sounds like inscription work, not data entry.
- It preserves the ritual feel of the interaction.

Lesson:
- Small wording changes can protect or break the whole frame.

### Avoid: answer-space that feels broader than the fiction

Observed in:
- ingredient-pair puzzles that admitted multiple sensible products
- clueing where the player's reasoning was good but the canonical was narrower than the story justified

Why it fails this taste:
- It feels less like archaeology and more like fighting a validator.

Preferred fix:
- tighten clueing
- accept alternatives
- or change the mechanic

### Avoid: a puzzle whose payload lands as trivia

Observed in:
- procedure/word-sequence mechanics that did not initially feel connected to the record they unlocked

Why it fails this taste:
- The player solves the puzzle but the story beat arrives detached from the act of solving.

Preferred fix:
- make the solve path echo the record's themes
- or bridge it explicitly in the reveal text

## How Agents Should Use This

When proposing a revision:

- Name the taste problem directly.
  Example: "house voice flattened," "reveal explains but does not recontextualize," "too much generic mystery phrasing."

- Preserve the mission's structural job.
  Do not improve the prose by deleting the reason the mission exists.

- Prefer small, high-leverage rewrites.
  One sentence that changes the interpretation is often better than a full rewrite.

- Offer changes in the house's voice.
  A good Jones fix should not sound like a good Drake fix.

- When rewriting a reveal, answer:
  what did the players discover, what does it now mean, and why does this house care?

- If uncertain, propose 2-3 variants with a note on the tradeoff.

## What To Add Over Time

This doc will get stronger if future revisions append:
- examples Anthony explicitly loves

- moments from live players that landed hard

The target is not to freeze taste. The target is to make it legible enough that agents stop making the same wrong guesses.
