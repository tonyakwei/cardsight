## 1. Per-History-Card Analysis

### Fragment: Clinic Record: First Exposure

**Summary:** A supervised introduction program is bringing young people — specifically a fifteen-year-old named Teren — into direct contact with the Source (called here "the bright chamber"). The effect is immediate and measurable: enhanced cognitive performance, increased focus. The cost is also immediate and measurable: loss of appetite, disrupted sleep, and a dependency response when stimulation is removed. A QRian official writes a formal recommendation that second visits be withheld until the dependency can be managed — not until it disappears, but until it can be tolerated without observable distress. This is a medical record in the same way a drug trial report is a medical record: it acknowledges the problem while continuing the program.

**Phase:** Early/blessing — this is the institutional period when Source exposure is still being managed with clinical language, supervised carefully, and reported through proper channels. It reads as early-phase because there is still a functioning recommendation system: something is flagged and a responsible adult writes it down. That infrastructure is still intact.

**Questions this raises:** Who authorized the supervised visit program? What is the "bright chamber" exactly — is it the Source itself or a controlled exposure environment? Why is age fifteen the entry point — is this a rite of passage or a practical threshold? Most importantly: did the recommendation (no second visit until dependency resolves) actually get followed? This fragment shows the problem being seen. It doesn't show what happened after.

### Fragment: The Fifty-Seventh Lesson

**Summary:** This is a teacher's record, written two generations after the Source was integrated into education. By now the effects are so profound that children are outpacing their adult teachers — entire generations of learned elders are being made obsolete by children who were exposed earlier. The writer feels both pride and shame. The pride is institutional: the valley schools are working. The shame is diagnostic: the writer has named something important — speed has been mistaken for wisdom. Cognitive acceleration is not the same as understanding, but the system has rewarded the acceleration so heavily that no one is stopping to ask whether the children have actually learned anything or are simply processing faster.

**Phase:** Growing dependency — two generations in. The Source is no longer an experiment; it's infrastructure. Education depends on it. The "fisherman's son" can derive sky-tables, which implies radical social leveling through cognitive enhancement. The writer's pride is genuine — this looks like flourishing. The shame is the tell: someone sees the gap between speed and wisdom, but the system has no mechanism to act on that observation.

**Questions this raises:** What is the "sky-table" — is this astronomical/mathematical knowledge, or something more specific to QRian lore? Is the fisherman's son reference meant to imply social democratization of knowledge, or is it specifically pointing toward people who would not normally be exposed to the Source? Two generations after schools opened — how much time has elapsed since First Exposure? Is "Teren" from the Clinic Record one generation earlier, or is this a different population entirely?

### Fragment: Kitchen Complaint from the Workers' Hall

**Summary:** This is a first-person account from someone working in a kitchen or food service context — a person close to the labor population, not the scholars. The workers are exhibiting obsessive mathematical/counting behavior: refusing to sit at a table of eight places when the party is seven, rejecting round bread for reasons that can only be geometric or numerical. They are polite, even grateful, but incapable of normal eating behavior. Their minds are elsewhere — specifically, with "the stones" (counting stones, likely connected to the Source or its associated tools). The writer's line "We are feeding the mind and forgetting the mouth" is a direct statement of what the Source's effect looks like from outside: cognitive capture at the cost of physical self-maintenance.

**Phase:** Obsession/contagion — this is the period where Source dependency has spread into the general labor population and is producing visible social dysfunction. The behavior is no longer confined to supervised academic contexts (as in the Clinic Record) or the educated class (as in the Fifty-Seventh Lesson). It has reached workers. And it is interfering with basic biological function.

**Questions this raises:** Is this happening in the same location as the Clinic Record and the Lesson, or has it spread to a different settlement? What are "the stones" specifically — are these a physical artifact connected to the Source, or a learned counting behavior that has become compulsive? The seven vs. eight places detail is specific — is there something numerologically significant about those numbers in QRian mathematics, or is this generic OCD-style pattern-rigidity? And critically: does anyone in authority see this? The kitchen worker is writing it down, but to whom?

### Fragment: Foreman Krane's Completion Record

**Summary:** This is a foreman's operational log from the final sealing of the temple. Krane records specific structural closures: a sealed choke-point, a false stair built to look older than it is, a lowered gate, and a set of design principles the council has explicitly mandated: no straight road in, no clear road out, no unwitnessed chamber openings. The deliberate incorporation of false architecture (the aged fake staircase) is particularly important — this is not just sealing, it's active deception. The note "if we build mercy into the plan, someone later will mistake it for an invitation" is the most unsettling single line in Drake's fragment set. Six laborers were lost during this day's work, recorded with the same register as the gate lowering. The work continues.

**Phase:** Sealing/end — this is the terminal phase. The temple is being deliberately made confusing, deadly, and resistant to re-entry by design. The council's authority is still functioning (orders are being followed), but the callousness toward the lost laborers and the explicit rejection of "mercy" as a design principle suggests the institutional priority has shifted from managing the crisis to erasing access.

**Questions this raises:** Who is on the council that issued these orders? Was the sealing done to protect the outside world from the Source (containment), or to protect the Source from the outside world (preservation)? Krane uses "we" — is this the same QRian institution, or a successor group cleaning up? What happened to the six laborers — were they lost in construction accidents, or were they deliberately kept inside? The phrase "no chamber that opens without witness" maps directly to the temple's architecture Drake encountered in Acts 1 and 2 — this is the document that explains why the temple is the way it is.

## 2. Reference Card Review

### Major Decisions

**Destroy the Source** (Drake's card)

This aligns cleanly with Drake's instinct. The Clinic Record shows the Source produces measurable, observable dependency in first exposure — at a supervised, controlled, single visit. The Kitchen Complaint shows it has spread to workers. The Fifty-Seventh Lesson shows it has been running uncontrolled through an educational system for two generations. The Foreman's Record shows even the people who understood it best chose to seal rather than study it further. Every fragment Drake holds points toward a substance/phenomenon that cannot be safely managed once contact is made. Drake's position: the Source is not dangerous because it is poorly understood. It is dangerous because it is *working*. Its effect — cognitive acceleration at the cost of normal function and social coherence — is reliable, consistent, and eventually unsurvivable as a civilization.

Destroying it ends the risk. It also ends the knowledge. Drake's Act 3 temptation (financial survival via "Save for Research") is real, but if Drake's instinct is "settle the threat, not postpone it," Destroy is the only internally consistent position.

**Recontain the Source** (Croft's card)

Drake will be skeptical. Recontainment is what the QRians attempted — and the temple is what that looks like from the other side. Krane's record is a recontainment record: seal the chamber, deceive future entrants, build no mercy. The result is a trap that has been catching expeditions for centuries. "Recontain" sounds like a resolution but it is actually just a longer-duration deferral. Drake's argument against it: if the QRians — who understood the Source better than anyone alive — decided recontainment was the safest available option, and we are now standing inside their recontained temple three acts later, the recontainment didn't work. It just postponed the problem until today.

**Open It for Research** (Jones's card)

The financial temptation is real. But the Clinic Record is a first-exposure record from a supervised program — and the subject showed dependency after a single visit. If a controlled first exposure in a managed academic setting produces that outcome, "controlled access for study" is a description of the first phase of QRian history. Drake knows how that ends because Drake just read the next three fragments. Open for Research is how you get to Kitchen Complaint.

### Settlement Clauses

**Drake would champion:**

- **No Private Keeping** — This is Drake's most important clause regardless of which major decision wins. Drake's own financial temptation is the strongest argument for it. If Drake wants to take relics for money, other factions will too, and some of them have more resources and less visibility. No Private Keeping forecloses the extraction economy before it starts.
- **Withhold the Mechanism** — If the major decision lands anywhere other than Destroy, this clause is essential. Sharing cultural findings is defensible; sharing the technical details of how Source exposure produces cognitive acceleration is handing the next generation a blueprint for the same catastrophe. This is Drake's concession clause: "fine, research/recontain, but not with a manual."
- **No Ready Passage** — This pairs with Foreman Krane's design philosophy. If the QRians built the difficulty into the architecture deliberately, the settlement should not undo it. Making re-entry difficult, costly, and deliberate is not the same as destroying the source, but it buys time between any future decision and its consequences.

**Drake would accept:**

- **Leave an Unmistakable Warning** (Croft's) — Consistent with Drake's "settle the threat, don't just defer it" instinct. If the Source is not destroyed, a legible warning is the minimum responsible action. The QRians left warnings, but they were encoded in a glyph system only specialists can read. "Unmistakable" is the operative word.
- **Three-Witness Rule** (Croft's) — If recontainment wins the major decision vote, this is a reasonable operational clause. Drake would accept it as a friction mechanism that slows future re-entry without Drake having to win the Destroy argument.
- **No Judgment Without Record** (Jones's) — Drake does not find this objectionable in principle — documenting before destroying is reasonable. However, Drake will want the record to not become an indefinite delay mechanism. If "full documentation" takes five years and the Source can affect anyone in proximity, the clause needs a time limit.

**Drake would oppose:**

- **Publish the Discovery** (Jones's) — Publishing what happened here invites future expeditions. If the major decision is Destroy, there is nothing left to publish about the Source's mechanism. If the major decision is Recontain or Research, publishing invites exactly the kind of private extraction that No Private Keeping is trying to prevent. The cultural history is fine. The "we found a Source" announcement is not. Drake sees this as Jones's way of keeping the option open for commercial or academic access indefinitely.
- **Preserve Some Artifacts** (Jones's) — Too vague. "Some artifacts" is a container for whatever Jones wants to take out. Without defining what counts as an artifact and what carries Source contamination risk, this clause is a blank check for the extraction economy. Drake would demand this clause either be struck or be paired with "No Private Keeping" and "Withhold the Mechanism" as hard conditions.
- **Copy the Inscriptions** (Jones's) — Neutral to slightly opposed. Copying inscriptions that explain the Source's mechanism is effectively the same as Withhold the Mechanism failing. If inscriptions are copied without restriction, the technical details survive even if the physical site is destroyed. This clause undermines Withhold the Mechanism unless scoped narrowly (cultural/historical inscriptions only, not technical).

## 3. Drake's Reconstructed Timeline (Best Attempt)

Working only from Drake's four fragments:

1. **Clinic Record: First Exposure** — earliest. This is the formal institutional beginning: supervised visits, clinical documentation, a recommendation system still functioning. The language is careful and professional. The problem is visible but contained.

2. **The Fifty-Seventh Lesson** — two generations after "first valley schools opened." So at minimum one generation after the Clinic Record (assuming the schools opened around or after the supervised visit program). The effects are now systemic and embedded in education. The writer sees the problem (speed vs. wisdom) but no one is acting on it.

3. **Kitchen Complaint from the Workers' Hall** — this is from "the period of first public disturbances," which implies the dependency has spread beyond the academic class. Workers are exhibiting full-spectrum behavioral dysfunction (refusal to eat, compulsive counting). The language is quieter than the Lesson writer but the observation is more alarming — this person is watching people starve.

4. **Foreman Krane's Completion Record** — last. The sealing is the terminal act. The council has made the decision; the foreman is executing it. Six workers were lost and the work continues. This is after the crisis has been decided upon, not during the crisis itself.

**Confidence levels:**
- Position 1 (Clinic Record): high. "Third season of supervised visits" indicates an early, still-experimental program. The clinical format and the active recommendation (not a post-hoc reflection) place this during active functioning of the institution.
- Position 4 (Foreman's Record): high. Terminal documents are identifiable — they describe completion, not process.
- Positions 2 and 3 (Lesson and Kitchen Complaint): medium confidence on the ordering between them. The Lesson is "two generations after schools opened" — that's a fixed temporal marker. The Kitchen Complaint is "from the period of first public disturbances" — that's relative to when disturbances began. If the schools produced cognitive acceleration that led directly to the labor population's exposure, the disturbances may have begun before the Lesson was written. Tentative ordering: Lesson before Kitchen Complaint (schools opened, then spread to workers), but this could flip if another house's fragments show a different sequence.

**What I cannot determine from Drake's four fragments alone:**
- Where the Source came from. None of Drake's fragments describe discovery or origin.
- What the Source physically is. "Bright chamber" is the only description, and it's atmospheric not physical.
- What the council was, how it decided on sealing, and what alternatives they considered.
- Whether the workers' dysfunction (Kitchen Complaint) led directly to the council sealing decision, or whether those are parallel tracks.
- The fate of the people inside when Krane sealed the temple. Six laborers were "lost" that day. Were others sealed in intentionally?

## 4. Drake's Opening Position for the Round Table

The temple is a record of a decision made under impossible conditions — and then made permanent in stone. We now know what that decision was: the QRians sealed the Source not because they were ignorant of its value, but because they had lived with that value for two generations and watched what it cost. By the time the fisherman's sons were outsmarting their teachers, workers were refusing to eat because the bread was round. The Clinic Record shows a fifteen-year-old who couldn't let go of a counting stone after a single supervised visit. That is not a side effect. That is the effect. The Source works exactly as advertised — it expands the mind — and the price it extracts for doing so is the rest of the person. The QRians built this place to make sure no one could walk back in casually. Foreman Krane was told to put a false staircase in so that future visitors couldn't trust what they saw. He was told not to build mercy into the plan because someone would mistake it for an invitation. That is what recontainment looks like from the inside. We are standing in the recontainment. It did not hold. Drake's position is this: we have better information than any of the three houses that came here before us, and the information says destroy it. If there is knowledge worth preserving — cultural records, inscriptions, artifacts that carry no Source contamination risk — we preserve those. But the mechanism itself, the thing that makes the bright chamber bright, ends here.

## 5. What Drake Needs from Other Houses

**Highest priority gaps:**

1. **Origin/discovery of the Source.** None of Drake's four fragments describe how the QRians found or created the Source. Drake knows the middle and end of the story (dependency, dysfunction, sealing) but not the beginning. Another house almost certainly holds a fragment that describes the Source's origin, discovery, or the moment of first deliberate contact. Without this, Drake cannot assess whether the Source was found, made, or attracted. That distinction matters: something made could potentially be unmade with the same knowledge; something found might have a location outside the temple; something attracted could still be active.

2. **What the Wall of Repetitions actually said.** The inscription the three houses spent Act 2 reconstructing — Drake knows it exists (one line is Drake's, one is Jones's, one is Croft's) but never saw the full three-line inscription. If that wall's message is a warning, a command, or a description, it may directly support or contradict Drake's Destroy position. Drake needs to know what the QRians were so desperate to preserve that they carved it hundreds of times.

3. **The council's deliberation or decision record.** Krane's foreman's log tells Drake that a council ordered the sealing. Drake has the execution record but not the decision record. Another house may hold a fragment that describes who the council was, what arguments were made, and whether Destroy was considered and rejected. If the QRians explicitly considered and rejected destroying the Source before sealing it, that is load-bearing information for Drake's argument.

4. **What "the Source" looks like physically.** "Bright chamber" is not enough. Is the Source an object, a location, a process, a living thing? The answer changes the feasibility of Drake's Destroy position — you can't destroy something you can't physically act on.

5. **Connection between Drevu and Togom (from Act 2) and the broader institutional fracture.** Drake's Act 2 fragments show two individuals hiding things from colleagues (Drevu's compartment, Togom's sealed bunker). If another house has fragments that explain what Drevu was hiding or what Togom sealed, it would help Drake understand whether the institutional fracture was about disagreement over the Source's danger, or about something else entirely.

## 6. Final Story Log Entry

Drake arrived in Act 3 with a theory of institutional collapse — a civilization that built a trap for itself. The four history fragments confirm and specify that theory. The Source is not mysterious; it is documented. It enhances cognition and it destroys social cohesion, physical self-maintenance, and eventually the ability to function outside its influence. The QRians knew this — they had a clinical record, a teacher's confession, a kitchen worker's testimony — and they sealed it anyway rather than destroy it, presumably because no one wanted to be the person who ended what the fisherman's son had become. Foreman Krane's record makes Drake's choice clear: the QRians built the trap to protect the future from the past. Drake's job is to decide whether the protection was sufficient or whether it needs to be made permanent. The moral charge of Act 1 (the trap was built to keep people in) and Act 2 (the institution fractured under pressure) resolves here into a single question: was the sealing a solution or just the best-available deferral? Drake's read: deferral. The settlement ends it.

## 7. Designer Flags

### Fragment ambiguity and clarity

**Clinic Record — "bright chamber":** The phrase does real atmospheric work but provides no physical information. Is the bright chamber a room, an artifact, an aperture? For Act 3 deliberation purposes, players need some mental model of what they're deciding to destroy or preserve. If the bright chamber is described in another house's fragments, this may be resolved in the round table. But if no fragment names the physical nature of the Source, all three houses are deliberating on destroying something they cannot picture. Consider adding one concrete sensory detail that suggests physical nature without over-describing.

**Kitchen Complaint — "the stones":** This is the second reference to counting stones (the Clinic Record mentions Teren couldn't let go of his counting stones). That connection is meaningful — counting stones are a physical artifact through which Source exposure is mediated. But the connection is never made explicit in Drake's materials. Players who notice both references will correctly infer the mechanism; players who don't will miss it. Decide whether this is an intentional inference puzzle or accidental fragmentation. If intentional, good — it rewards careful reading. If accidental, add a brief connector.

**Foreman Krane's Completion Record — "six laborers were lost":** "Lost" is ambiguous. Construction accident (they fell), deliberate sacrifice (they were sealed in), or something Source-related (they wandered off and couldn't be recovered). The matter-of-fact register suggests construction accident, but in this context everything else is deliberate. If this is a designer choice (some things in the record are morally monstrous, presented flatly), it works well and is unsettling in exactly the right way. If it's accidental ambiguity, clarify. The flat register is the scariest version — keep it, but know it will be discussed at the table.

**The Fifty-Seventh Lesson — "speed has been mistaken for wisdom":** This is the only fragment in Drake's set that includes self-aware moral commentary from within the QRian system. The other three are observational or operational. This one is analytical. That makes it tonally distinct, which is fine (different authors, different genres of writing), but it should be intentional. If all twelve fragments have a similar distribution of observational/analytical/operational tones, this is good variety. If most fragments are purely observational and this one stands out, consider whether the analysis level is correct for the character and moment.

### Reference card clarity

**"Preserve Some Artifacts" — needs scoping language.** As written, this clause is a blank invitation to extract whatever a house decides to call an artifact. If the clause is intentionally broad (to create negotiation tension), fine — but flag it as intentionally broad in the host notes so the host knows to let the argument happen. If it's meant to be scoped (e.g., cultural artifacts only, not Source-adjacent materials), the text needs one more sentence.

**"No Judgment Without Record" — needs a time constraint.** "Before any irreversible decision is enacted, the chamber must be fully documented." This is reasonable but it creates an indefinite delay loop if documentation takes longer than available time or resources. As a clause in a timed game setting this works because the host can rule on it. As a real-world clause in a settlement, it's an indefinite blocking mechanism. Consider whether players will catch this tension and if the designer intends it.

**"Open It for Research" — the Jones major decision card doesn't describe what "controlled access" means or who controls it.** The other two major decision cards (Destroy, Recontain) describe an action and a trade-off. "Open It for Research" describes a permission and a qualification ("controlled," "present generation") without specifying who sets the controls or what happens when the present generation's controlled access ends. This makes it harder to argue against than the other two, because it sounds moderate. Consider whether that's intentional (Jones should be the most persuasive option to balance the deliberation) or an imbalance.

### Act 1+2 carry-forward payoffs

**The Wall of Repetitions inscription — not resolved.** Drake spent significant effort in Act 2 reconstructing one line of the three-part inscription. The inscription presumably says something important. Drake never sees the full three-line text in this packet. This is correct design (full text emerges in round-table pooling), but the payoff requires the host to actively surface the inscription text during deliberation. If Jones and Croft don't bring it up, Drake's Act 2 mechanical achievement (the majority-vote reconstruction) produces no narrative dividend. Flag this for the host: prompt the table to share their Wall of Repetitions results early in Act 3.

**"The Source" was never named in Drake's materials through Acts 1-2.** As flagged in the Act 2 designer summary, Drake's materials gestured toward a threat without naming it. The Act 3 Fifty-Seventh Lesson is the first Drake fragment to use the word "Source" explicitly. That payoff lands — the Act 2 gap is resolved here. This is good pacing. However, it means Drake's position through most of Acts 1-2 was evidence-based but not label-anchored. Teams that played carefully will feel the recognition; teams that weren't tracking the theory may need a brief host recap before Act 3 begins.

**The "armory hypothesis" (Drake's Act 2 dominant theory) — partially resolved.** In Act 2, Drake theorized the Reagent Alcove was an armory — compounds prepared to fight the Source. This is never directly confirmed in Act 3's Drake fragments. The Kitchen Complaint and Clinic Record suggest the QRians were responding to the Source's effects on their people, which is consistent with defensive preparation. But Drake's specific "armory" reading (weapons against the Source) is not confirmed, denied, or deepened by Act 3 fragments. Other houses' fragments may resolve this. Flag it as a round-table question: "What were the compounds in the Reagent Alcove actually for?"

**Drevu and Togom — not referenced in Act 3 fragments.** The two named individuals who hid things from colleagues in Act 2 (Drevu with his compartment, Togom with his bunker) do not appear in Drake's Act 3 materials. It's possible they appear in other houses' fragments or that their identities are left deliberately unresolved. If the intent is that Drevu and Togom were individuals who knew more than they let on (perhaps they understood the Source's danger before the consensus did), their silence in Act 3 is a gap. Consider whether to add a brief footnote reference in another house's fragment that ties them to the sealing decision — something that would emerge in round-table pooling.

### Pacing estimate

A 5-7 person Drake team, reading all four history fragments carefully, reviewing the reference cards, and discussing before the round table: **20-30 minutes.** The fragments are not puzzle-dense — there is no extraction or cipher work. The work is interpretive: what does this tell us, when did it happen, what does it mean for the vote. The four fragments have a natural reading order (clinic record → lesson → kitchen → foreman), and the reference cards are self-explanatory. The main discussion driver will be the Destroy vs. Recontain argument (Drake's card vs. Croft's card) and how much Drake actually wants to push Destroy given the financial temptation of Research. Experienced discussion groups will have a clear position in 20 minutes; groups that need to re-read and re-debate will take 30. The deliberation phase (round table with all houses) should budget additional time on top of this.
