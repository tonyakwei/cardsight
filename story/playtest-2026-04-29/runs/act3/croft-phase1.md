# Croft Company — Act 3 Phase 1 (Solo Analysis)

---

## Section 1: Per-History-Card Analysis

---

### Fragment: Letter from a Schoolmaster
*"From the first year of the valley schools"*

**Summary:** A schoolmaster describes children from farms solving advanced proofs in months rather than years, and a student publicly correcting his star-table. He reports that the Survey Council calls the Source "a blessing from the earth" and begins to agree. This is an entirely positive account — no alarm, no ambivalence. The schoolmaster is impressed and relieved. The acceleration of learning is visible, concrete, and attributed without hesitation to proximity to the Source.

**Phase:** Early/blessing. This is the period when the effect of the Source is experienced as gift. The institutional framing ("the council calls it a blessing") indicates this view is official and shared, not idiosyncratic. There is no friction in this document.

**Questions this raises:**
- How long after initial discovery of the Source is "the first year of the valley schools"? Is this the generation that built the temple, or a later one that inherited it?
- Are the "valley schools" located inside the temple, near the Source, or nearby but separate? The schoolmaster sees the children accelerate but doesn't describe going to a chamber — implying proximity may be ambient or via scheduled visits rather than physical immersion.
- Is Mila a named character who appears in other fragments? A student who corrects the star-table at this early stage and could surface as a named adult in later fragments would be strong continuity.
- The council already has a name for the Source ("a blessing from the earth") at this stage. When was the Source discovered, and who named it?

---

### Fragment: Order Limiting Second Visits
*"Issued after repeat visits became common"*

**Summary:** The Survey Council issues a rule preventing citizens from re-entering "the bright chamber" more than once per ten-day, unless summoned for official purposes. The stated justification is not punishment but correction — too many people are trying to return before they've even processed the first visit. The document is bureaucratic in tone: official, brief, mildly paternalistic, not alarmed. The Council is managing demand, not danger.

**Phase:** Growing dependency. This is the first administrative signal that exposure is creating a pull. People want to return, and the pull is strong enough to require a rule. However, the Council frames this as a management problem (too many visits, work not being recorded) rather than a medical or moral one. The framing is still positive-adjacent: the problem is that people love it too much, not that it's hurting them.

**Questions this raises:**
- "The bright chamber" — is this an interior name for the chamber where the Source is housed? This is the first specific architectural reference to where the Source is experienced. Does this name appear in other fragments?
- The "measuring office" is listed alongside schools as an institution that gets priority access. What does the measuring office measure? Given the Reckoning Floor (Act 2) tracked a count of time, a "measuring office" may be the institution responsible for that work. Connecting these would give the Source's bureaucratic infrastructure more coherence.
- Who issued this order — a council functionary, or someone with higher authority? The absence of a named author is notable given other fragments are signed. Anonymous official orders suggest either institutional authority or a later period when individuals are less prominent in the record.
- What happens when people violate the rule? No enforcement mechanism is mentioned. Is this absence relevant?

---

### Fragment: Letter of Priest-Scientist Ennar
*"Written after the first routes were closed"*

**Summary:** Ennar describes students solving in one season what used to take twenty years, repeats the "blessing" framing from the schoolmaster period — and then pivots. She found a student (Tarek) still at his work after sunset, after moonrise, and through to dawn. He had not eaten. When she touched him, he wept as if being pulled from prayer. She names the condition clearly: "We are becoming excellent at the cost of becoming unable to stop." This is the inflection point in the arc — the first fragment where the cost of the Source's effect is named by someone who is observing it from outside.

**Phase:** Obsession/contagion. This is the transition fragment. Ennar begins in the blessing-language (one season vs. twenty years) and ends in the damage-language (unable to stop). The pivot is a single observed incident — one student at one tablet. The document shows the institutional label ("blessing") surviving contact with direct observation, but only barely. Ennar does not revoke the blessing framing; she layers damage onto it. The condition is described as an addiction without using that word.

**Questions this raises:**
- "After the first routes were closed" — what routes? This implies some partial access restriction has already happened between the Order Limiting Second Visits (institutional management) and this letter (routes closed). The progression seems to be: rules → route closures → full sealing. What prompted the route closures?
- Ennar's title is "Priest-Scientist" — a compound role. This is the only indication that the QRians had a religious dimension to their relationship with the Source. The schoolmaster and the council order are secular. Ennar's role suggests the Source had theological significance to someone. Was it contested? Did priests and scientists disagree?
- Tarek is named. Does he appear in later fragments as a figure who sealed something, fled, or wrote a warning?
- Ennar's phrase "dragging him from prayer" is not metaphorical — she means it literally. At what point did Source-proximity become framed as religious practice rather than intellectual activity?

---

### Fragment: The Last Copyist's Oath
*"From the final months before the upper archive was closed"*

**Summary:** Nine copyists remain in the upper archive. Three can no longer hold a stylus steady — motor function is failing. They are shifting from stone to thinner clay to make room for grain and seal-tools, implying resource reallocation away from knowledge preservation and toward basic survival. The document ends as a formal oath: they did not hide the truth out of fear, but because they feared leaving behind only a warning and a locked door. They wanted to leave more than that.

**Phase:** Sealing/end. This is the terminal document. The copyists are the last people working on preservation. The physical deterioration (hands that can't hold a stylus) is the first direct mention of Source-caused physical harm, not just cognitive compulsion. The resource reallocation (stone shelves to grain and seal-tools) indicates the broader QRian community is no longer prioritizing knowledge preservation — survival has displaced it. The oath itself is an act of institutional self-justification: the copyists want to be understood, by whoever reads this later, as people who made a choice rather than simply failed.

**Questions this raises:**
- The oath says "we hid the truth because we feared leaving nothing behind except a warning and a locked door." This is a direct reference to what the temple actually is from the outside — a warning and a locked door. The copyists anticipated this outcome and wanted to resist it. Did they succeed? Did the inscriptions they copied survive?
- "Three can no longer hold a stylus steady" — is this the same deterioration Ennar observed in Tarek (unable to stop), or a further stage? Tarek couldn't stop working; these copyists can't work at all. If these are the same people a generation later, the progression is: compulsion → dysfunction.
- Who are the nine? Any names that cross-reference to other fragments?
- The shift from stone to clay is materially significant — clay is less durable, more fragile, more subject to water damage. Is the flooding mechanism (Act 1) designed to destroy these clay copies? Or to preserve them (sealed in water, anaerobic)?
- "Before the upper archive was closed" — this is the same archive described in Croft's Act 2 brief as the Green Department (the upper temple). The closing of the upper archive is the same event as the temple's sealing sequence in Act 2. This fragment and the Act 2 physical experience are the same moment from different perspectives.

## Section 2: Reference Card Review

---

### Major Decision Cards

**Destroy the Source (Drake)**
Drake's position. Ends the danger by eliminating the Source entirely, at the cost of all future knowledge and access. This is the irreversible option. From Croft's perspective: this is too final. The QRians' records survive; their knowledge is not lost (assuming the copyists succeeded). But the Source itself carries value — evidence of something real about cognition and place and the relationship between minds and materials. Destroying it forfeits the ability to study what actually happened and why. Croft's primary objection: destruction is not stewardship, it's abandonment of responsibility.

**Recontain the Source (Croft)**
Croft's assigned position. Restore containment; leave it closed. This is the middle path — neither destroy nor open. From Croft's perspective: this honors the QRians' own final choice (they sealed it) while acknowledging that the world outside deserves protection. It preserves optionality. Future generations who are better-equipped — better-warned, more careful, with better tools — could choose differently. Croft would open the deliberation with this and hold it firmly unless presented with a compelling reason to abandon it.

**Open It for Research (Jones)**
Jones's position. Controlled access, present generation. From Croft's perspective: this is the position Croft has to resist hardest, because of internal house pressure. Lara Croft would want this. The team's loyalty to Lara creates a real pull toward Jones's option. But the fragments are explicit: people who were smart, systematic, and self-aware were still consumed. The Order Limiting Second Visits shows that even the QRians couldn't manage exposure safely after a certain point. "Controlled access" is what the Survey Council said when they issued the ten-day rule. They didn't control it. Croft's counter: the QRians had all the advantages (they built the place, they understood the Source's mechanism, they had institutional infrastructure) and still failed to contain the cognitive effects. We have none of those advantages.

**Croft's honest stance:** Recontain, with resistance to Open It for Research on the grounds that the QRians' own failure to manage exposure is the main piece of evidence. This is not panic (Destroy) and not overcaution (pre-emptive Open It) — it's following what the evidence actually says the QRians themselves concluded.

---

### Settlement Clause Cards — Croft's Position

**Clauses Croft holds and would champion:**

**Leave an Unmistakable Warning** — Croft's highest priority clause. The Last Copyist's Oath is exactly about this: the copyists feared leaving only a warning and a locked door, and wanted to leave more. Croft can quote this fragment directly. The QRians failed to leave a warning clear enough for the outside world. Correcting that failure is the minimum obligation of everyone in this room.

**Three-House Custody** — Strong support. No single house should control what survives. Croft's own instinct ("the question is what should be left unopened and how clearly you owe that warning to whoever comes next") is institutional, not proprietary. Shared custody removes the incentive for one house to re-enter for competitive reasons.

**Three-Witness Rule** — Croft would champion this. It structurally prevents any future unilateral access. Given that one of the main ways Source-exposure cascades is through individual obsession (Tarek, the copyists' deteriorating hands), requiring multiple parties from multiple houses for any contact creates a check on individual obsession. It's also consistent with Croft's Act 3 stewardship framing.

**No House Takes Credit** — Croft supports this. It follows from Croft's identity (loyalty to Lara's mission, but not blind; awareness that the patron's obsession is itself a risk). Claiming singular credit creates incentives to control access — which conflicts with Three-House Custody.

**Clauses from other houses Croft would support:**

**No Judgment Without Record (Jones)** — Croft would support this actively. It requires full documentation before any irreversible action. Croft's Act 2 analysis consistently pushed for understanding before action; this clause operationalizes that. It also delays any decision to Destroy until documentation is complete, which Croft prefers.

**Withhold the Mechanism (Drake)** — Croft would support this. Publish the history and culture; redact how the Source works. This is consistent with "Leave an Unmistakable Warning" while preventing a future bad actor from reverse-engineering the effect. The warning should be about what the Source does to people, not a technical manual for how to build one.

**No Ready Passage (Drake)** — Croft would support this as a physical correlate to Recontain. If the Source is recontained, making return difficult and costly is the material implementation of that choice. This clause is the physical enforcement of Croft's major decision.

**Clauses Croft would oppose:**

**Publish the Discovery (Jones)** — Croft would oppose this or heavily qualify it. The risk: publishing the discovery tells the world a navigable temple exists with a chamber that accelerates cognition. This creates a race condition — multiple expeditions, governments, bad actors — that the QRians' own cautionary arc says ends badly. Croft's position: publish the cultural and historical findings (already covered by Withhold the Mechanism), but suppress the location (Drake's Suppress the Location clause).

**Suppress the Location (Drake)** — Croft has ambivalence. If Publish the Discovery is off the table, Suppress the Location works in combination with Leave an Unmistakable Warning. But suppression is a fragile mechanism (someone finds the location anyway; the warning has no context). The combination Croft prefers: Leave an Unmistakable Warning in a widely-distributed form that does not include location specifics.

**Copy the Inscriptions (Jones)** — Croft has no objection in principle. The Last Copyist's Oath shows the QRians already tried this and partly succeeded. Copying the inscriptions honors that work. Croft would accept this clause but not champion it — it's a lower priority than Warning and Custody.

**Preserve Some Artifacts (Jones)** — Croft is wary. "Selected physical artifacts" is ambiguous — selected by whom, by what criteria, stored where? If artifacts are cognitively active (i.e., proximity to them triggers the same effects as the Source), then artifact preservation is Source exposure by another name. Croft would want a clause that subordinates this to "artifacts verified as inert." As written, Croft would push back on this or request amendment.

**No Private Keeping (Drake)** — Croft supports this in spirit but it overlaps heavily with Three-House Custody and No House Takes Credit. If those two pass, No Private Keeping is redundant. Croft would suggest it as a backup if the custody clauses fail to land.

## Section 3: Croft's Reconstructed Timeline (Best Attempt)

Based on internal evidence in Croft's four fragments alone, without access to other houses' fragments:

**Position 1 — Letter from a Schoolmaster** (confident)
"From the first year of the valley schools" is the explicit temporal marker. This is the earliest institutional response to the Source — the schools were created to channel the acceleration effect. The tone is unambiguous blessing. The Council already has a name for the Source. This is after discovery but before management problems.

**Position 2 — Order Limiting Second Visits** (confident)
"Issued after repeat visits became common" dates it to after the schools were established and the Source was in regular use. The problem being managed (too many return visits) is a second-order effect of widespread institutional use — it requires a large enough population of Source-users that "repeat visits became common" as a pattern. The tone is still managerial, not alarmed. This follows the schoolmaster period but precedes the route closures.

**Position 3 — Letter of Priest-Scientist Ennar** (medium confidence)
"Written after the first routes were closed" places this after some partial access restrictions that are not represented in Croft's cards. The schoolmaster and the Order are both before Ennar; Ennar writes after some routes (but not all) have been closed. The deterioration she observes (Tarek) is individual and recent — she's just found it, it's the worst thing she's seen, and she hasn't named it as systemic yet. This positions Ennar's letter at the early stage of the obsession/damage period — routes are being managed, but individual deterioration is only beginning to appear in the professional class.

**Position 4 — The Last Copyist's Oath** (confident)
"From the final months before the upper archive was closed" is an explicit near-terminal marker. This is the last documented period before the sealing. The physical deterioration (hands that can't hold a stylus), the resource reallocation (stone shelves to grain), and the nine-person remnant all indicate a community near its end. This is definitively the latest of the four.

**Full Croft-internal sequence: Schoolmaster → Order Limiting Visits → Ennar → Last Copyist's Oath**

**Confidence assessment:**
- Start (Schoolmaster) and end (Last Copyist) are high confidence — both have explicit temporal markers.
- Middle two (Order → Ennar) are medium confidence on relative order. Order Limiting Visits could plausibly be contemporaneous with Ennar's letter rather than before it — a route closure could have prompted both the Order and Ennar's concerns at the same time. However, the Order's tone (bureaucratic management, not alarm) is more consistent with a period before Ennar's observed crisis than during it.

**What Croft cannot determine without other houses' fragments:**
- Where in the 12-fragment arc these four land. Croft's four may constitute fragments 1–2–3–9 (early, early, early-mid, late) or 2–4–6–10 — no way to know without the other eight fragments.
- Whether there are fragments covering: the initial discovery of the Source; the construction of the temple itself; the mechanisms Krane built (flood, tidal cycle); the specific event or decision to seal the upper archive; any personal testimony from Krane or Yenus (who appear in Act 2 but not in Croft's Act 3 fragments); the period between Ennar's crisis observation and the Last Copyist's terminal period.
- Whether there is a fragment from someone directly experiencing the obsession rather than observing it (all four of Croft's fragments are written by observers — schoolmaster watching students, council watching citizens, Ennar watching Tarek, copyists watching themselves failing).

## Section 4: Croft's Opening Position for the Round Table

Croft's opening statement for the cross-house deliberation:

The QRians left us a manual for what happens when you do not Recontain. The Schoolmaster wrote it in year one — children learning in months what took his best students years, and he called it a blessing. The Survey Council wrote the next chapter — too many people couldn't stop returning to the chamber, so they issued rules. Ennar wrote the chapter after that — a student she found at his tablet all night, unable to eat or stop or leave, weeping when she touched him, and she named it: we are becoming excellent at the cost of becoming unable to stop. The last chapter was written by nine copyists whose hands were shaking, who knew the archive was closing, who copied what they could onto thinner clay because the stone shelves were needed for grain. Every chapter is the same story told by people who understood what was happening and could not prevent it even with warnings, rules, and closed routes. Our position is Recontain: leave it sealed, as the QRians left it sealed, but correct the one thing they failed to do — leave a warning clear enough that the next generation of people who find this place knows what they are looking at before they open it. We are asking for three clauses: an unmistakable warning, three-house custody of whatever survives, and a three-witness rule for any future contact. Those three things together say: we were here, we saw this, we are not hiding it, and we are not handing it to any one person or house to manage alone.

## Section 5: What Croft Needs from Other Houses

**From Drake (whose position is Destroy):**
- What evidence does Drake hold that argues for irreversibility? Croft's four fragments show escalating damage but not catastrophic or irreversible harm in the short term. If Drake has a fragment showing mass casualties, a total collapse, or something that could not be walked back, that is the evidence that would move Croft from Recontain toward Destroy.
- Who or what is Krane in Drake's Act 3 fragments? Krane appears in Act 2 (Croft's Mission 3) as the architect who sealed his own working notes. If Drake holds Krane's own Act 3 testimony or a document about the sealing decision, that is crucial for understanding whether the QRians' sealing was a managed choice or a desperate last act.
- Does Drake have a fragment from inside the damage arc — someone experiencing the compulsion, not observing it? Croft's fragments are all observer-perspective. A fragment from inside the experience (the student at the tablet; the copyist who can no longer hold the stylus steadily but still cannot stop) would change the texture of the deliberation.

**From Jones (whose position is Open It for Research):**
- What evidence does Jones hold that argues for the Source being manageable or reversible? The Order Limiting Second Visits shows the QRians thought it was manageable. Was there a period where the rules worked? If Jones has a fragment from a period where management succeeded, even briefly, that is the evidence that would most challenge Croft's Recontain position.
- Does Jones have any fragment about Yenus? Croft could not reach Yenus's ledge in Act 2 due to the Act 1 lockout — Yenus's personal theory is the largest gap in Croft's understanding. Yenus hid his theory above flood level, expecting a future visitor with the means to climb. If any house holds Yenus's fragment, Croft wants to read it in Act 3 before the deliberation closes.
- Does Jones have any fragment about the construction period — who built the flood mechanism, and why? Croft's theory (from Act 2 Mission 3, Krane's working notes) is that the flood and tidal mechanisms were designed containment structures. If Jones has a document from the construction period that confirms or contradicts this, it bears directly on whether Recontain is viable (i.e., whether the original containment actually worked).
- What is the discovery that Jones holds about the "measuring office"? The Order Limiting Second Visits mentions schools and the measuring office as institutions with priority access. The Reckoning Floor (Act 2 Mission 1) is almost certainly the measuring office's instrument. If Jones has a fragment from the measuring office's perspective, it could clarify what the QRians were counting down to — the Reckoning — and whether it is still relevant.

**The single most important missing piece:** Yenus's personal theory. Croft holds Riddle-Tablet I and could not reach the ledge. If any house has a fragment that is Yenus's theory, or a document that summarizes it, that is the evidence Croft most needs for Act 3. Everything else is inferential; Yenus apparently had a direct thesis and hid it deliberately for a capable future reader. Not knowing it is the wound Croft carries from Act 1.

## Section 6: Final Story Log Entry

Croft enters the final chamber knowing the QRians were systematic, self-aware, and still consumed — and having the evidence, from their own hand, of exactly how it happened. The four fragments form a clean arc from blessing to oath, and the arc is conclusive: proximity to the Source erodes volition progressively, even for people who name the damage while it's happening. The open question from Act 2 — why did they stay, why did they seal rather than flee — is partially answered by the Last Copyist's Oath: they stayed to leave something behind besides a warning and a locked door, and they feared that would be all anyone found. What Croft cannot know is whether they succeeded, and what Yenus believed — the one QRian individual who hid his conclusions above flood level for a future reader who never came, because Croft lost the rigging in Act 1. That gap will either be filled by another house's fragments or will remain the thing Croft carries into the vote: the sense that the most important testimony in the archive is the one we cannot reach.

## Section 7: Designer Flags

### Fragment-level ambiguities

**"Letter from a Schoolmaster"** — No designer issues. Clean, clear, well-placed temporally. The signal ("blessing") is unambiguous. Star-table detail is good grounding. Minor: Mila is named but will likely be forgotten unless she appears in another fragment; if she does not, naming her has no mechanical payoff. Either cut the name or use her in a later fragment.

**"Order Limiting Second Visits"** — The author is anonymous (Survey Council, no named signatory). All other fragments are signed by named individuals. This is either a deliberate design choice (institutional anonymity vs. personal voice) or a consistency issue. Recommend deciding intentionally: if anonymous institutional orders are a design choice (to contrast with personal letters), it works. If it's an oversight, add a name.

The phrase "too many who leave the chamber ask to return before their work is even copied down" is slightly ambiguous — does "their work" mean the work they did in the chamber (they didn't write down the insights from the visit), or the work they do in their profession (they're not copying documents at their regular jobs)? In context the first reading is almost certainly intended, but the second reading is available and could confuse players. Recommend: "...ask to return before even writing down what they received."

**"Letter of Priest-Scientist Ennar"** — This is the strongest individual fragment. The pivot from "blessing" to "weeping as if dragged from prayer" is a good escalation beat. The "Priest-Scientist" title implies a dual role that is narratively interesting — but it's the only hint of religious framing in the entire Croft packet, and it's load-bearing for understanding the Source's cultural significance. If other fragments don't develop the religious dimension, the title is doing a lot of work with no backup. Recommend: one additional mention of the religious framing somewhere (a reference in another fragment to "the rites of the bright chamber" or similar) to make it land as cultural detail rather than isolated vocabulary.

**"The Last Copyist's Oath"** — The shift from stone to clay ("stone shelves must be given to grain and seal-tools") is an excellent concrete detail. The phrase "we hid it because we feared leaving nothing behind except a warning and a locked door" is the most emotionally resonant line in the Croft packet and possibly in the whole act — it names exactly what the temple is from the outside and reframes the sealing as a choice made in grief, not in failure. This line is load-bearing for Act 3 emotional stakes. If players don't reach it, the deliberation loses its best anchor. Make sure this fragment reaches every house.

One structural issue: "nine of us left in the upper archive, and three can no longer hold a stylus steady." The physical harm (shaking hands, motor deterioration) is introduced here and only here. This is a significant escalation from Ennar's cognitive compulsion (unable to stop) to physical incapacity. The jump from "can't stop thinking" to "hands don't work" is large and underexplained. Is motor deterioration a later-stage symptom? A separate effect? Players will ask. Either a brief note in the fragment ("the shaking comes after the sleeplessness, which comes after the visits — we know the sequence now") or a separate fragment covering the physical deterioration arc would help.

### Reference card / clause issues

**Preserve Some Artifacts (Jones)** — As written, this clause is open-ended in a way that creates a gameplay problem: "selected physical artifacts" is not defined. In a real deliberation, this will generate extended debate about which artifacts and how they're stored, which is fine if the designer wants that debate, and is a distraction if the designer wants a clean yes/no clause vote. Recommend adding a modifier: "Preserve some physical artifacts, to be held in three-house custody under the terms of Three-House Custody." This links two clauses and makes the scope concrete.

**Suppress the Location vs. Leave an Unmistakable Warning** — These two clauses are in partial tension. A warning tells people there is a danger, which may prompt them to go looking. Suppressing the location prevents that. But if both clauses pass and the location leaks anyway (or is found by another expedition), the warning exists and the location is findable — the suppression failed and the warning only draws attention. The clauses are not incompatible (warn without specifying location; suppress location while maintaining the warning) but in the heat of negotiation players may see them as mutually exclusive. A design note or slight rewording to make the compatible reading clear would reduce confusion.

**No Judgment Without Record (Jones)** — This is procedurally sound but has an edge case: if the chamber is actively deteriorating or dangerous and players are still inside it when the deliberation is happening, "full documentation before any irreversible decision" may be physically impossible in the time available. In the game's context, Act 3 is already the "final chamber" with still air and stopped doors — so the urgency pressure is narrative, not mechanical. But the clause should clarify whether "fully documented" means "documented to the best of current ability" or "documented to a completeness standard that could take months." As written, a player arguing for Destroy could claim this clause blocks them indefinitely. Recommend: "documented to the extent possible before departure."

### Act 1+2 carry-forward payoffs and gaps

**Paid off fully:**
- The Wall of Repetitions (Act 2 Mission 4) framing — "wisdom turning to obsession slowly enough to write down" — is the emotional and analytical precursor to reading Ennar's fragment. Act 2 told players what the phenomenon looked like; Act 3's Ennar fragment shows it happening to a specific person. The payoff is clean.
- Krane's sealed compartment (Act 2 Mission 3) established that someone designed the flooding and sealing mechanisms deliberately. The Last Copyist's Oath confirms the sealing was a final decision made under duress, not a mechanical default. These two pieces together close the "why did they seal it" question.
- The Reckoning Floor's answer (TIME, Act 2 Mission 1) is extended by the Order Limiting Second Visits — people were being measured against a clock, and they were also being rationed against a calendar. Two different kinds of time-management running simultaneously.

**Not paid off:**
- Yenus. The entire Act 2 lockout arc (Mission 5) positions Yenus's hidden theory as the most important inaccessible testimony. Act 3's Croft fragments have no Yenus material. If Jones or Drake also have no Yenus fragments, the thread is a dead end that the game raised to prominence (Croft holds Riddle-Tablet I and went to the ledge and couldn't reach it) and then dropped. This would be the most significant unresolved thread.
- The Source is never named or described in Croft's Act 3 fragments. The Act 2 analysis identified this gap (the Source is never named or described directly in the Croft packet). It is still true in Act 3. Players who played Acts 1 and 2 with Croft have an understanding built through inference but no direct statement from the QRians about what the Source actually is — physical object, place, phenomenon, or something else. The deliberation ("what to do about the Source") is harder to anchor when the Source has no physical description.
- Mila (the student who corrected the star-table). She is named and smart and could have become a named adult voice in a later fragment. She does not appear again. This is not a structural problem, just a small lost opportunity.

### Pacing estimate

A 5-7 person Croft team reading this packet: four history fragments (2 pages total) + one major decision + five clause cards + the reference card list (12 clauses). Total reading volume is modest — 10-15 minutes to read everything once.

Discussion time is the variable:
- Fragment discussion: 8-12 minutes (the Ennar and Last Copyist fragments will generate the most discussion; Schoolmaster and Order are quickly processed)
- Timeline reconstruction: 5-10 minutes (Croft's four fragments have enough explicit temporal markers that confident ordering takes 5 minutes; confirming the order against internal evidence adds 5 more)
- Clause review and position formation: 10-15 minutes (12 clauses, some of which are obviously aligned or opposed with Croft's instinct, some of which require debate)
- Opening statement drafting: 5-8 minutes

**Total Phase 1 time estimate: 28-45 minutes for a real 5-7 person team.** This is appropriate for an act that culminates in a deliberation rather than puzzle-solving. If Phase 2 (cross-house round table) is 20-40 minutes, total Act 3 is 50-85 minutes — at the high end, but this is the final act and the deliberation is the content.

The packet is well-scoped. The main pacing risk is the clause review: 12 clauses is a lot to evaluate and advocate for in a time-pressured setting. Players will likely default to reading their own four clauses and the major decisions, and treat the full 12-clause review as optional. That's probably fine, but if there are clauses in other houses' hands that Croft would benefit from knowing about, the designer should ensure Phase 2 explicitly includes a clause-sharing beat before voting begins.
