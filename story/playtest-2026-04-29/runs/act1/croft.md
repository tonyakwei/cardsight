# Croft Company — Act 1 Playtest

## Theory of the temple — pre-game (just from story brief)

The QRians built this temple with intent — the flooding reads as designed, not accidental. The corridors that tilt at wrong angles and the stone heavier than it should be suggest construction beyond normal engineering. The "script no one has deciphered" on the walls and objects scattered on the floor point to a civilization that embedded information into the physical environment deliberately. Working theory: the temple is a test or record-keeping system, and the flood is part of its mechanism to filter or pressure those who enter.

### Mission 1: Recover Your Grappling Rigs

**Final answer:** 5372146 2 (i.e., 5 3 7 1 4 6 2)

**Working:** All 3 Unmarked Steel Hardware cards are in Croft's hands — no trading needed. All 7 items present. Rigging sequence derived from the descriptive language in each item's tag:

- #5 Wall Studs: "First into bare rock. Nothing holds without them." — explicit "first," goes 1st.
- #3 Anchor Plate: "Bolts flush to the starting anchor for a tie-off point." — anchor set after studs are in, 2nd.
- #7 Fixed Line: "Ties off at the base, hangs the full height." — line goes up after anchor is set, 3rd.
- #1 Rope Clamps: "Bite the rope going up." — clamps require the fixed line to exist, 4th.
- #4 Haul Pulley: "Mounts at the top to run the haul line through." — requires someone to have climbed to the top first, 5th.
- #6 Top Bolts: "Expansion bolts for the alcove. Useless until someone climbs up." — explicitly contingent on a climb happening first, 6th.
- #2 Cargo Hook: "The last thing on the line." — explicitly last, 7th.

**Final answer:** 5 3 7 1 4 6 2

**Confidence:** medium
**Difficulty:** 3
**Time-to-solve guess:** 12–18 minutes for a 5-7 person team
**Solving mode:** understood-mechanic
**Sticking points:**

1. The puzzle asks for "assembly order" but the tags say they're "inventory labels only" and "do not indicate rigging order" — correct framing that sets up the challenge. However, this disclaimer creates a mental hurdle: players may spend time wondering if there's a hidden numbering system before engaging with the descriptions.

2. The ordering of #4 (Haul Pulley) and #6 (Top Bolts) is the hardest call. Both require someone to be at the top. The distinction is: the pulley guides the haul line (operational step), while the top bolts secure the alcove anchor (structural step that enables the haul). A real rigger might argue top bolts go before the haul pulley. The text for #6 says "Useless until someone climbs up" — which implies #6 is placed DURING or AFTER climbing, not before the pulley. But the pulley is also mounted at the top. This pair's relative order is genuinely ambiguous from the card text.

3. #3 Anchor Plate vs #7 Fixed Line: "starting anchor tie-off" vs "ties off at the base." Both reference the base. The distinction is that the plate is the bracket the line attaches to, so plate must precede line — this is inferable but requires domain knowledge about rigging mechanics.

**Felt earned?:** partial — the explicit markers (#5 "first," #2 "last") anchor the sequence nicely at both ends. The middle is a reasoning chain that works but has an ambiguous node (#4 vs #6).

**Designer flags:**

- The #4/#6 ordering problem needs resolution. Either make one tag more explicit about which comes first, or accept that either order should be marked correct. Currently a real team could convincingly argue 5 3 7 1 6 4 2 — "top bolts first to secure the alcove, then thread the haul pulley."
- The description of #4 says "Wheeled block" (the card text in croft.md has a typo: "Wheels block" — missing "ed" on Wheeled). Minor, but if printed on a physical card this reads oddly.
- No trading required is good for a first mission — grounds the team in the mechanic before cross-house dependency.

### Mission 2: Solve the Stone Jigsaw

**Final answer:** STONE

**Working:** All 3 Odd Edge-Marked Block cards are in Croft's hands — no trading needed. All 5 tiles present.

Tiles:
- Tile 1: Left ☆, Right ●, Letter S
- Tile 2: Left ●, Right ▲, Letter T
- Tile 3: Left ▲, Right ◆, Letter O
- Tile 4: Left ◆, Right ◗, Letter N
- Tile 5: Left ◗, Right ♦, Letter E

Chaining rule: right edge of tile N must match left edge of tile N+1.

Chain: Tile 1 (right=●) → Tile 2 (left=●, right=▲) → Tile 3 (left=▲, right=◆) → Tile 4 (left=◆, right=◗) → Tile 5 (left=◗, right=♦)

This chain is unique — no ambiguity, only one valid ordering.

Letters in order: S, T, O, N, E = **STONE**

**Confidence:** high
**Difficulty:** 1
**Time-to-solve guess:** 4–6 minutes
**Solving mode:** understood-mechanic
**Sticking points:** None. The chain logic is clean, the symbols are distinct, the answer is a real word.

**Felt earned?:** yes — satisfying, physical-feeling puzzle. Putting tiles in a line feels like the real action even when done on paper.

**Designer flags:**

- Trivially easy for a full team once the chain logic clicks (roughly 90 seconds of actual puzzle time after the "aha"). This is fine as a palate cleanser or warm-up, but it won't slow a team down at all. If this is meant to have teeth, add a false-start possibility (e.g., one symbol ambiguously matches two different edges).
- The fact that all 5 tiles are in Croft's hands when the set is marked as "3 cards" means the tiles are split across 3 physical card scans (2+2+1), not 3 separate tiles given to 3 houses. Confirm this is intentional and that real play doesn't require inter-house trading for Mission 2. If it is intentional (all tiles with Croft), the "some in your hands, others pocketed" flavor text is misleading — it primes players to expect a trade that won't happen.
- **Critical flag:** If the design intent is that the five tiles are distributed across all three houses (one card per house), the current card data gives all five tiles to Croft in their starting hand. That's either correct (no trade needed) or a data error. Needs verification against the seed.

### Mission 3: Reach the Impossible Vase

**Final answer:** 6 3 8 5 1 7 4 9 2

**Working:** All 3 Inscribed Metal Spoke cards are in Croft's hands — no trading needed. All 9 spokes with translations provided directly on the cards (glyph → English translation pairs given explicitly).

Words: 1=FIND, 2=EVER, 3=WHO, 4=WAY, 5=WILL, 6=THOSE, 7=NO, 8=ENTER, 9=OUT

Task: arrange into a sentence, then enter spoke numbers in sentence order (top to bottom = the sentence read naturally).

Available words: FIND EVER WHO WAY WILL THOSE NO ENTER OUT

Sentence construction: "THOSE WHO ENTER WILL FIND NO WAY OUT EVER"

Mapping to spoke numbers:
- THOSE = spoke 6
- WHO = spoke 3
- ENTER = spoke 8
- WILL = spoke 5
- FIND = spoke 1
- NO = spoke 7
- WAY = spoke 4
- OUT = spoke 9
- EVER = spoke 2

**Final answer:** 6 3 8 5 1 7 4 9 2

**Confidence:** high
**Difficulty:** 2
**Time-to-solve guess:** 8–12 minutes
**Solving mode:** understood-mechanic
**Sticking points:**

1. The glyph translations are given directly on the cards — players don't decode anything, they just get the words. The puzzle is pure anagrammatic sentence-construction. This is fine but it means the "glyph system" scaffolding does no work here — the translation is handed to you.

2. "THOSE WHO ENTER WILL FIND NO WAY OUT EVER" is the obvious sentence, but a team might debate: "THOSE WHO WILL ENTER FIND NO WAY EVER OUT" or similar permutations. The answer feels strongly canonical but anagram puzzles always carry a low residual ambiguity risk.

3. The instruction says "top to bottom, the inscriptions read a sentence" — meaning the SPOKES are to be physically placed top-to-bottom, and reading them in that direction gives the sentence. So the FIRST spoke placed (top) = first word of the sentence. This is clear once re-read carefully, but the phrase "enter the spoke numbers in sentence order" could be read as "list the numbers 1–9 in the order they'd appear in the sentence" which is what I've done — or as something else. Low confusion risk but worth auditing in player testing.

**Felt earned?:** yes — the sentence lands with appropriate ominousness. The QRians built a warning into their climbing footholds. Good thematic punch.

**Designer flags:**

- Again, all 3 cards are in Croft's hands but the flavor text says "others picked up by the teams around you." If this is intentional (no trade needed), the flavor is misleading again. See flag on Mission 2.
- The glyph translation is given on the card rather than requiring players to use a separate glyph key. This is appropriate for Act 1 (onboarding), but the puzzle is somewhat thin as a result — word-unscrambling only. Consider whether Act 2 equivalents ask players to decode rather than just rearrange.
- Sentence is unique enough that it likely has one canonical answer, but stress-test against: "WILL THOSE WHO ENTER FIND NO WAY OUT EVER" (grammatical variant). The puzzle needs a tiebreaker if multiple grammatical orderings exist.

### Mission 4: Activate the Teaching Stone

**Final answer:** 13 23 38

**Working:** All 3 Numbered Bone Token cards are in Croft's hands — no trading needed. Missing inner values revealed:

- Position 3: inner = 5
- Position 5: inner = 9
- Position 8: inner = 15

Full inner ring: 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15 — arithmetic sequence, difference of 2 (odd numbers).

Known outer values: 3, 8, ?, 18, ?, 28, 33, ?

Testing linear formula Outer = a × Inner + b:
- From position 1: 3 = a(1) + b
- From position 2: 8 = a(3) + b
- Subtracting: 5 = 2a → a = 2.5, b = 0.5

Verification:
- Position 4: 2.5(7) + 0.5 = 18.0 ✓
- Position 6: 2.5(11) + 0.5 = 28.0 ✓
- Position 7: 2.5(13) + 0.5 = 33.0 ✓

Solving for unknowns:
- Position 3: 2.5(5) + 0.5 = **13**
- Position 5: 2.5(9) + 0.5 = **23**
- Position 8: 2.5(15) + 0.5 = **38**

**Confidence:** high
**Difficulty:** 2
**Time-to-solve guess:** 10–15 minutes
**Solving mode:** understood-mechanic
**Sticking points:**

1. The formula Outer = 2.5 × Inner + 0.5 works cleanly but uses non-integer coefficients. Players who try to find the pattern by looking at outer values alone (3, 8, ?, 18, ?, 28, 33, ?) and not relating them to inner values will likely attempt arithmetic sequences on the outer ring directly. The outer-only differences are: 5, ?, 10, ?, 10, 5, ? — irregular. This dead end could frustrate teams for several minutes before they think to use Inner as the independent variable.

2. The formula is Outer = (5 × Inner + 1) / 2 — which might be more intuitive expressed as a ratio rule if the QRians used a base-5 or fractional system. As presented it's a purely mathematical pattern without thematic grounding. Teams that don't think in algebra may brute-force this.

3. The puzzle instructions say "Discover the pattern in the inner ring, then find the rule that transforms inner values to outer values." This is good scaffolding — it explicitly tells players to use the inner ring first. But players who find the inner ring pattern (odd numbers) and then try "outer = inner × 3" (which gives 3, 9, 15, 21... and doesn't match) will hit frustration before landing on 2.5.

**Felt earned?:** partial — the math works and is satisfying once found, but 2.5 as a coefficient feels slightly arbitrary without thematic justification. If the QRians' number system is base-5 this would land better with a note.

**Designer flags:**

- The non-integer coefficient (2.5) is the puzzle's main difficulty spike. If a real team doesn't have someone comfortable with linear equations from two data points, they may stall. Consider whether this difficulty level is appropriate for Act 1 or whether it belongs in Act 2.
- Alternative: make the formula Outer = Inner × 2 + (position number) or some other rule that's discoverable through table inspection rather than algebraic solving. The current formula is clean mathematically but not discoverable by pattern-spotting alone.
- The "Teaching Stone" flavor is thematically resonant — the QRians literally used this as a number lesson. The puzzle mechanic matches the narrative (a pedagogical device). Good.
- No trading required is again a design choice that conflicts with the flavor text. Verify whether the bone tokens should be distributed across houses.

### Mission 5: Follow the Drag Marks

**Final answer:** TELL MY CHILD I LOVED THEM STILL

**Working:** All 3 Ancient Pottery Shard cards are in Croft's hands — no trading needed.

The wall inscription is given as `{{{TELL MY CHILD I LOVED THEM STILL}}}` — meaning these words are written in QRian glyphs, and the pottery shards provide the decryption key.

Glyph-to-letter mappings across the three shards:
- Large shard: T, E, L, M
- Crooked shard: C, H, I, D
- Painted shard: O, V, S, Y

Letters needed for "TELL MY CHILD I LOVED THEM STILL": T, E, L, L, M, Y, C, H, I, L, D, I, L, O, V, E, D, T, H, E, M, S, T, I, L, L

All letters (T, E, L, M, C, H, I, D, O, V, S, Y) are covered across the three shards. No gaps. No trading required.

**Confidence:** high
**Difficulty:** 1
**Time-to-solve guess:** 5–8 minutes (includes the emotional processing of the content)
**Solving mode:** understood-mechanic
**Sticking points:**

1. Mechanically trivial — the glyph translations are handed to players on the cards, and in the playtest file the inscription is already given in English notation inside the `{{{ }}}` markers. In actual play with physical cards, players would need to decode the glyph sequence character by character. The difficulty depends entirely on whether the physical glyph font is legible and whether the shard cards' pairings are visually clear.

2. The answer IS the inscription — there's no transformation or manipulation. Players who expect a puzzle may feel this is too direct. The puzzle IS the decoding, and the reward is the human moment of reading what someone wrote.

**Felt earned?:** yes — this is the most narratively powerful moment in Act 1. A dying person's last words decoded through their civilization's teaching tools. The puzzle is light but the payload is heavy. That's correct calibration.

**Designer flags:**

- This mission depends entirely on the physical glyph rendering. If `{{{TELL MY CHILD I LOVED THEM STILL}}}` renders as actual glyphs in the game's font system, players have to decode letter by letter using the shard keys. If it renders as readable text, the mission is trivially solved without the shards. Confirm that the glyph font is active and rendering correctly before playtesting.
- The pottery shard cards are labeled "teaching aids" by the QRians — this is elegant. The artifact's in-world purpose matches its game-mechanical purpose.
- The decoded message "TELL MY CHILD I LOVED THEM STILL" has strong emotional weight and will land well in a room of players. It's the humanizing beat of Act 1.
- All three shards being in Croft's hands again raises the same flag as Missions 1–3: is the inter-house trading dynamic working? Five of five missions require no trading if the card distribution in the seed gives Croft all cards for all their missions. This needs verification against the other houses' packets.

Based on the card data as presented in the briefing file, **Croft holds all required cards for all five missions** — no trading required. However, the flavor text of every mission says pieces are "in others' hands" and must be traded for. This is a significant discrepancy that needs design review.

If the intent is that trading IS required and the briefing file represents all cards (not just Croft's starting hand), then the distribution is:

- Unmarked Steel Hardware × 3 — all in Croft hands (Mission 1 requires 3)
- Odd Edge-Marked Block × 3 — all in Croft hands (Mission 2 requires 3)
- Inscribed Metal Spoke × 3 — all in Croft hands (Mission 3 requires 3)
- Numbered Bone Token × 3 — all in Croft hands (Mission 4 requires 3)

If instead the briefing file ONLY shows Croft's starting cards and other houses hold additional cards from the same sets — meaning each required set of 3 has some cards with Croft and some with Drake/Jones — then trading is necessary and the file only shows Croft's partial holdings. This interpretation is more consistent with the mission flavor text and the 54-card-across-3-houses setup.

Under the second interpretation, cards needed from trading would be determined by which house holds the cards Croft doesn't have. Since I can't read the other houses' packets, I cannot determine specifics. However: if each card set requires 3 cards and Croft already holds all 3 of each set, no trading is needed.

**This is the single most important structural flag for the designer to verify.**

## End-of-act theory of the temple

The Temple of the QRians was built by a civilization of systematic, pedagogically-minded people who encoded knowledge into every physical surface and object. They used mathematics (the Teaching Stone's formula), sequence logic (the rigging assembly, the tile chain), linguistic systems (the glyph language), and physical barriers (the edge-coded stone seal) as interlocking tools of both record-keeping and instruction. This was not a warehouse or a tomb — it was a school, or a test.

The flooding is designed. The water rises through channels that "seem almost designed for it" (host narration). The QRians built the flood mechanism into the architecture — this is a pressure system that forces visitors to act quickly, prioritize, and demonstrate competence. The stone that seals in Act 2 suggests the temple has multiple stages of progressive closure.

Someone died here — dragged, imprisoned in a sealed chamber, writing a final message to their child in QRian glyphs. This means at least one QRian (or QRian-literate person) was killed or imprisoned inside the temple by someone else. The temple's mechanisms may have been used against its own people, or factions within QRian society turned on each other.

The warning inscribed on the climbing spokes — "Those who enter will find no way out ever" — was placed by the QRians themselves on their own climbing tools. This is either: (a) a warning they left for future visitors knowing the temple would trap them, or (b) documentation of what had already happened to them. Either way, the QRians knew the temple was a trap, and they stayed to document it.

Working hypothesis: the QRians built something here that became uncontrollable — the "Source" referenced in game materials — and the temple was designed to contain it, or to contain knowledge of it. The flood, the sealing, the progressive closure across acts — these are containment mechanisms. The QRians died or disappeared inside their own containment structure. We are now inside it.

## Designer summary (most important section)

### Hardest puzzles

1. **Mission 4 (Teaching Stone)** — the hardest by a significant margin. Non-integer linear formula (Outer = 2.5 × Inner + 0.5) is not pattern-discoverable through table inspection alone; requires algebraic reasoning. A team without one algebraically-minded player may stall for 15+ minutes or not solve it at all.

2. **Mission 1 (Grappling Rigs)** — medium difficulty. The #4/#6 ordering is genuinely ambiguous from the card text. Both "top bolts before pulley" and "pulley before top bolts" are defensible. This will cause unnecessary argument and may result in teams submitting a wrong answer confidently.

3. **Mission 3 (Inscribed Spokes)** — light difficulty. The sentence is unique and the words are given. Risk: teams may spend time debating grammatical variants if the canonical sentence isn't locked.

### Puzzles with answer/clue/mechanic problems

- **Mission 1:** #4/#6 ordering ambiguity — needs one tag rewritten to make relative sequence unambiguous. Also: minor typo "Wheels block" should be "Wheeled block."
- **Mission 4:** 2.5 coefficient is mathematically correct but experientially inelegant for Act 1. Recommend either: (a) adjust the outer values to use a whole-number formula, or (b) move this puzzle to Act 2 where players are more warmed up.
- **Mission 5:** Depends on whether the glyph font renders correctly. If `{{{ }}}` content shows as readable English in actual play (not as glyphs), the mission is a no-op. Must verify physical rendering.
- **All missions:** The flavor text says pieces are scattered across houses, but the briefing shows Croft holding all cards for all missions. Either the distribution is wrong (all cards need trading to work) or the flavor is misleading. This is the top structural risk.

### Puzzles that worked well

- **Mission 2 (Stone Jigsaw):** Clean, satisfying, zero ambiguity. The chain-matching mechanic is physical and intuitive. STONE as the answer is a good payoff. Good warm-up puzzle.
- **Mission 5 (Drag Marks):** Light puzzle, heavy narrative payload. Correct calibration. The pottery shard "teaching aids" matching their in-game purpose is elegant design.
- **Mission 3 (Inscribed Spokes):** The inscribed warning landing as a complete threatening sentence is the best thematic moment in Act 1 (alongside Mission 5). The sentence "Those who enter will find no way out ever" is memorable and tonally right.

### Story coherence

The Act 1 missions form a coherent picture: a civilization of systematic, pedagogically-minded builders who encoded knowledge into physical objects, built a flood-triggered containment structure, and left at least one person to die inside it. The progression from "assemble your kit" → "seal the breach" → "decode the warning" → "solve the lesson" → "find the last words" tracks well. By the end, players have a lived theory of the QRians.

One gap: the missions don't yet explain WHY the temple floods or who is responsible. The host narration says "someone's entrance caused this, or the temple did" — but after five missions, we still don't know which. This is appropriate for Act 1 if Act 2 resolves it, but players may want a hint.

### Pacing

A real team of 5-7 will likely:
- Breeze through Mission 2 (4-6 min) and Mission 5 (5-8 min)
- Take moderate time on Mission 3 (8-12 min) and Mission 1 (12-18 min)
- Potentially stall on Mission 4 (10-20+ min depending on math comfort)

Total estimated act time: 45-70 minutes, with Mission 4 as the primary variable. If Mission 4 is meant to be a bottleneck, it's doing its job. If it's meant to be approachable Act 1 content, the formula needs adjustment.

The trading dynamic is untested because the card distribution as shown requires no trading. If trading is required (correct design intent), the actual play time will be longer due to cross-house negotiation.
