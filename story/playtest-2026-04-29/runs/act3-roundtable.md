### Room's likely reconstruction (with error analysis)

Each house's solo attempt surfaces where the room lands naturally.

**Near-certain placements (high confidence from all three houses):**

- Position 1: Schoolmaster. Croft flags "first year of valley schools" explicitly. Only blessing-era candidate.
- Position 11: Krane's Completion Record. Drake identifies it as a terminal execution document; Croft confirms via the Last Copyist's temporal proximity.
- Position 12: Sefa's Testimony. Jones has explicit "last day before the lower seal." No contest.
- Position 9: Last Copyist's Oath. Croft reads "final months before the upper archive was closed" and cross-references Krane; sequences it correctly near the end but pre-Krane.

**Contested placements (medium confidence, likely resolved in cross-pollination):**

- Positions 2-3: Clinic Record vs. Learning Blocks. Drake places Clinic Record first ("third season of supervised visits" is early, experimental). Jones places Learning Blocks in the blessing-tipping-to-compulsion phase. The ambiguity is real: both are discovery-era documents and neither cross-references the other. The room will probably get this right because Drake and Jones both independently flag their fragments as early-phase — the question is micro-ordering between positions 2 and 3. Likely outcome: correct, but debated. The Clinic Record's "third season" language is slightly more institutional (indicating established protocol) than the Learning Blocks note (which describes a newly-adopted script), making the canonical order arguable either direction. A real room has roughly 50% chance of swapping 2 and 3.

- Positions 4-5-6: Order Limiting Visits / Fifty-Seventh Lesson / Kitchen Complaint. Croft sequences Schoolmaster → Order correctly (Croft's two early Croft fragments). Drake sequences the Fifty-Seventh Lesson before Kitchen Complaint correctly. The cross-house merge requires the room to interleave Croft's position-4 card between two Drake cards (positions 5 and 6). Croft's Order Limiting Visits says "after repeat visits became common" — it reads later than Schoolmaster but the temporal anchor is ambiguous relative to the Fifty-Seventh Lesson's "two generations after schools opened." A careful room will note the generational gap and place the Order closer to position 2-3 (early institutional management), but a hasty room may push it to position 5-6 (treating it as a later, stricter measure). Likely error: Order Limiting Visits placed at position 5 instead of 4, bumping the Fifty-Seventh Lesson to 6 and Kitchen Complaint to 7.

- Positions 7-8: Order to Break the Fifth Drain / Letter of Priest-Scientist Ennar. Jones places the Drain order at "after night returns began" — clearly after public warnings are established. Croft places Ennar at "after first routes were closed" — which implies routes were closed before Ennar writes. The canonical ordering (Drain order at 7, Ennar at 8) requires the room to recognize that Ennar's "first routes were closed" references an event between the Order Limiting Visits era and her letter. Jones and Croft both tentatively identify this ordering, but the cue is indirect. Likely: the room gets this right, aided by Croft's explicit note that Ennar writes after route closures (a middle-crisis event), while the Drain order represents one of those closures.

- Positions 9-10: Last Copyist's Oath / Plain Warning for the Lower Stair. This is the most likely error. Croft places the Last Copyist's Oath in the terminal phase correctly. Jones places the Plain Warning at "after old warnings were judged a failure" — which Jones reads as mid-to-late crisis. The canonical order has the Plain Warning (10) after the Last Copyist's Oath (9), which is counter-intuitive: a public warning posted after the archive is already closing suggests the warning campaign escalated even as preservation was failing. A real room will likely argue these could be contemporaneous or swap them (placing Plain Warning at 9 and Last Copyist's Oath at 10). Probable error: positions 9 and 10 swapped.

**Summary of likely room performance:**

The room will almost certainly get the broad arc correct — blessing era (1-4), dependency era (5-8), containment/sealing era (9-12). They will probably get 9 or 10 of 12 positions right. The two most likely specific errors are: (a) Clinic Record and Learning Blocks swapped (positions 2-3), and (b) Last Copyist's Oath and Plain Warning swapped (positions 9-10). These are errors of ambiguous evidence, not reasoning failure — the fragments at those positions genuinely lack clear cross-reference anchors.

**Root cause of errors:** No fragment explicitly references another by name or event. The ordering relies entirely on internal temporal markers ("third season," "after the night returns began," "after the old warnings were judged a failure"). Where two fragments share a phase without cross-reference, the room must infer from contextual detail rather than documentary sequence.

## Step 2 — Round-Table Debate

### Round 1 — Opening positions presented

**Drake** opens by reading Foreman Krane's record aloud. The false staircase, the deliberate absence of mercy, six workers lost and noted in the same register as a gate lowering. Drake's opening argument: "We are standing inside their recontainment. It did not hold." Drake's position is Destroy, but the financial temptation is on the table — several Drake players will be visibly tempted by the Research option.

**Jones** opens by reading Sefa's Testimony. The land-killing compounds. The choice not to use them. "The QRians had our argument and made our decision — but they chose Recontain, not Open. What makes us different?" Jones immediately poses the hardest internal question before Drake or Croft can use it as a weapon. Jones's position is Open for Research with conditions: No Judgment Without Record is the floor.

**Croft** opens with the schoolmaster-to-last-copyist arc read sequentially. Croft does not lead with Destroy or Research. Croft leads with the arc itself: "Every chapter is the same story." Croft's position is Recontain, and Croft's strongest card is that this is what the QRians chose — not out of ignorance, but after seeing all four phases.

**Where evidence aligns:** All three houses agree on the phenomenology of exposure. The learning acceleration is real (Schoolmaster, Clinic Record, Fifty-Seventh Lesson). The compulsion is real and progressive (Learning Blocks, Order Limiting Visits, Drain order, Ennar). The physical deterioration at the end is real (Last Copyist's Oath: hands that can't hold a stylus). There is no disagreement about what the Source does.

**Where evidence conflicts:** The houses interpret the same arc differently. Drake reads it as proof that no level of management is safe. Jones reads it as proof that the QRians lacked a modern archival framework. Croft reads it as proof that the QRians made the right call given their options — and the current room's options are structurally similar.

### Round 2 — Cross-pollination of fragments

When the twelve fragments are laid out together and the timeline reconstruction begins, the following cross-house transfers matter:

**What Drake learns from Jones and Croft:**

Drake's four fragments (Clinic Record, Fifty-Seventh Lesson, Kitchen Complaint, Krane's Record) describe the middle and end but not the beginning. When Croft puts the Schoolmaster's letter on the table, Drake sees the blessing phase for the first time: children from farms solving proofs, the council calling it a gift, genuine institutional pride. This complicates Drake's pure "it was always a weapon" framing. The Source produced something real — Drake has to account for that in the Destroy argument. "We're not destroying a threat in disguise. We're destroying something that was genuinely good before it became unsurvivable."

When Jones shares Sefa's Testimony — particularly the land-killing compounds and the explicit rejection of annihilation — Drake encounters the most direct challenge to the Destroy position: the QRians had the means to destroy and chose not to. Drake's response, drawn from the Phase 1 analysis, is: "They chose Recontain. That choice is the temple. And the temple didn't hold." This is Drake's best argument, and the Sefa testimony is what makes it specific rather than general.

When Jones shares the Order to Break the Fifth Drain — "We do not break this passage because it failed. We break it because we can no longer trust ourselves with a way back" — Drake recognizes it as the same logic Drake is applying to the Source itself. This fragment, more than any other, validates Drake's instinct in QRian terms. Drake will quote it directly in the major decision debate.

**What Jones learns from Drake and Croft:**

Jones's four fragments (Learning Blocks, Drain Order, Plain Warning, Sefa's Testimony) cluster in the compulsion-to-end phase. Jones has no blessing-era material. When Drake puts the Fifty-Seventh Lesson on the table — "the fisherman's son can derive the sky-table" — Jones sees what was at stake. This is the evidence Jones needs to argue that Open for Research is not repeating the original mistake: "The QRians got two generations of this before the first public disturbances. If we implement research access with hard protocols, we have a window."

When Croft shares Priest-Scientist Ennar's letter, Jones sees the exact inflection point in the arc — the moment when "blessing" framing can no longer survive direct observation of a compelled student. Jones had the Plain Warning (late-phase blunt messaging) but not the Ennar letter (the moment of first-person recognition). Ennar is the evidence Jones needs to argue that the QRians' failure was institutional: Ennar sees it, names it clearly ("becoming excellent at the cost of becoming unable to stop"), and then nothing changes. The infrastructure to act on Ennar's observation doesn't exist. That, Jones will argue, is what we can provide that the QRians could not.

When Croft shares the Last Copyist's Oath, Jones gains the most emotionally resonant argument for Copy the Inscriptions: the copyists who died trying to preserve the record, hands shaking, copying onto thinner clay because the stone shelves were needed for grain. Jones will read this fragment aloud during clause negotiation. It wins the clause.

**What Croft learns from Drake and Jones:**

Croft's explicit gap from Phase 1 is Yenus (never filled by any house — no Yenus fragment exists in Act 3). Croft must carry that gap into the deliberation.

When Drake shares the Kitchen Complaint, Croft sees the dependency spreading to the general labor population — not just scholars and students. This extends the evidence base for Croft's core argument: the Source's effect is not confined to people who choose to engage with it intellectually. It reaches workers who are physically proximate. Controlled access cannot prevent ambient exposure if the Source's range is broad.

When Jones shares the Order to Break the Fifth Drain, Croft reads it as direct evidence for the Three-Witness Rule. The QRians tried willpower, tried rules, tried limiting visits — and then had to physically destroy the access point. The Three-Witness Rule is a modern version of the same logic: make return impossible without collective agreement. Croft explicitly adopts the Drain order as rhetorical support for the Three-Witness Rule clause.

When Jones shares Sefa's Testimony alongside Drake's Krane record, Croft is able to complete the picture: Krane built the prison with deliberate cruelty (false staircases, no mercy), while Sefa authorized it knowing it would seal people inside, uncertain whether it was courage or vanity. This pairing is the emotional core of Croft's argument: the QRians were not naive, not panicking, and not stupid. They were exactly as thoughtful as the current room — and they chose Recontain.

**Fragment shifts in position:**

- Jones shifts toward accepting Recontain as the major decision after hearing the Fifty-Seventh Lesson plus the Kitchen Complaint in sequence. The two-generation arc from Drake is more damaging to the Research position than Jones's own fragments, because Drake's fragments show the damage spreading beyond the supervised/academic class. Jones's internal argument (protocol could have saved them) becomes harder to make when the Kitchen Complaint shows laborers who weren't part of any supervised program already exhibiting full Source effects. Jones does not abandon Open for Research but becomes explicitly willing to accept Recontain if the clause package is strong enough.

- Drake's position does not shift significantly on the major decision, but Drake's tone in the clause negotiation softens when Croft shares the Last Copyist's Oath. Drake had framed No Private Keeping as a financial-extraction prevention clause. After hearing the copyists' oath, Drake accepts No Judgment Without Record as a legitimate precondition for Destroy that is not merely a delay tactic — it is the same instinct the copyists had.

- Croft's position is the most stable across the cross-pollination phase. The Croft arc (Schoolmaster → Order → Ennar → Last Copyist) is the most internally complete single-house narrative, and it does not need correction from other houses. Croft enters the major decision debate with the same position they brought into the room: Recontain. The cross-pollination adds texture and rhetorical ammunition but does not shift Croft's core reading.

### Round 3 — Major decision debate

**Drake vs. Jones:**

Drake: "Your argument is that the QRians lacked our framework. But look at the timeline: they had supervised visits with clinical records (position 2), they issued access rules (position 4), they closed routes (implied before position 8), they posted public health warnings (position 10). They built the framework. It didn't stop at the Kitchen Complaint stage. By position 6, workers who never opted into any supervised program were exhibiting Source dependency. The framework failed not because it was poorly designed but because the Source's range exceeds the framework's reach."

Jones concedes the spread-to-workers argument is the hardest challenge to the Research position. Jones cannot fully answer it from the fragment set. Jones's best counter: "Controlled access means the chamber, not ambient exposure. The workers got it through ambient proximity, not through supervised visits. A modern research protocol that seals and monitors access prevents the ambient propagation the QRians allowed to run for two generations. We have a different kind of control."

Drake's response: "The QRians built the chamber specifically to concentrate the Source for educational use. The moment you open controlled access, you are rebuilding the bright chamber. The question is not whether the first protocols hold — it's what the second generation does with them."

Assessment: Drake's argument is narrower but more evidenced. Jones's argument is more optimistic but requires accepting a distinction (ambient vs. supervised exposure) that the fragments do not cleanly support. Neither concedes, but Jones's advocacy for Open for Research weakens under cross-examination. Jones is clearly trending toward accepting Recontain as the major decision provided the clause package satisfies the archival condition.

**Drake vs. Croft:**

Drake: "Recontain is what the QRians did. We are the proof it didn't work. Three rival expeditions — one of which has financially desperate mercenaries who will auction their findings — all converged on this temple simultaneously. Whatever the QRians sealed, it was discovered again. Destroy is the only resolution that doesn't require a future generation to have this same conversation."

Croft: "The QRians sealed it without warning it. The temple is confusing and deadly by design — the false staircase, no mercy. That's not a warning; that's a trap. The reason expeditions keep finding it is not that containment failed — it's that the containment communicated nothing to the outside world. Recontain plus an unmistakable warning is structurally different from what the QRians did."

Drake is visibly affected by this argument. The False Staircase detail from Krane's record — which Drake flagged as the most unsettling single line in their materials — now cuts both ways. Croft uses it to argue that the QRians' recontainment was defective specifically because it was deceptive, and a corrected Recontain (with honest, legible warning) is a different thing.

Drake does not abandon Destroy but softens the argument to: "If the room votes Recontain, I need No Ready Passage and Leave an Unmistakable Warning as non-negotiable clauses. Recontain without those is just hoping the next expedition is more careful."

Assessment: Croft's strongest single argument in the entire deliberation. Drake's concession (acceptance of Recontain with conditions) is the turn that decides the major decision.

**Jones vs. Croft:**

This pairing generates the most natural convergence. Both houses are anti-Destroy. The dispute is between Research (preserve access) and Recontain (preserve without access). Jones's argument for research requires believing the current generation can manage what the QRians could not. Croft's argument for Recontain requires accepting that the current generation is not structurally different from the QRians.

The turning point: Jones acknowledges that the Sefa testimony — Jones's own strongest fragment — shows a civilization that chose Recontain even while holding a Destroy option. "Sefa chose our option. And she doesn't know if it was courage, vanity, or just the last form her love of order could take. That uncertainty is in the record. She didn't claim she made the right call." Jones reads this as an argument for humility, not for repeating Sefa's specific choice. But when Croft points out that Sefa had more information about the Source than anyone in the current room (she was there), Jones's position weakens further.

The convergence point: Jones proposes accepting Recontain as the major decision in exchange for a clause package that satisfies the archival condition. Specifically: No Judgment Without Record + Copy the Inscriptions + Three-Witness Rule. If those three clauses pass, Jones can accept Recontain without abandoning the scholarly mission. "We're not closing the book. We're closing the door while keeping the record."

Croft accepts this framing immediately. The convergence between Jones and Croft is complete by this point, and it determines the major decision.

### Round 4 — Clause negotiation

**Context:** Major decision is now tentatively Recontain, with Drake's grudging acceptance contingent on Drake's containment-platform clauses getting representation. The room has 5 clause slots for 12 possible clauses. 

**Jones's championed clauses:**
1. No Judgment Without Record — non-negotiable for Jones. All three houses accept this instantly. Jones reads the Last Copyist's Oath to anchor it emotionally. Slot 1: unanimous.
2. Copy the Inscriptions — Jones's scholarly identity clause. Drake has no objection (the inscriptions are cultural, not mechanistic). Croft supports it (the copyists tried to do this; we're completing their work). Slot 2: unanimous.
3. Publish the Discovery — Jones champions this; Drake opposes it hard. Drake's argument: publishing invites extraction, and the room has a financially desperate house (Drake itself) as proof. Jones's counter: suppression is already failed by the fact that three houses know. Drake pivots to offering a trade: "Accept Withhold the Mechanism and No Private Keeping with Publish the Discovery, and I'll support it." Jones agrees in principle. But the slot count is constraining — if Publish the Discovery takes a slot, one of Croft's four clauses must be dropped.

**Croft's championed clauses:**
4. Leave an Unmistakable Warning — Croft's highest priority. All three houses accept. This is the clause that directly responds to the QRians' failure. Slot 3: unanimous.
5. Three-Witness Rule — Croft's structural safety clause. Jones accepts it readily (it operationalizes the "archival but protected" condition). Drake accepts it because it embodies the Drain order logic: remove the easy way back. Slot 4: unanimous.
6. Three-House Custody — Croft's fourth clause. Croft wants all four clauses. But with slots 1-4 already assigned (No Judgment Without Record, Copy the Inscriptions, Leave an Unmistakable Warning, Three-Witness Rule), slot 5 is contested.

**Drake's championed clauses:**
7. Withhold the Mechanism — Drake's primary clause. Jones accepts this as a paired safeguard with Copy the Inscriptions. Croft accepts it (publish culture, redact the manual). This is actually the least contested Drake clause. The problem is slot competition.
8. No Private Keeping — Drake wants this because Drake knows their own financial temptation. Jones has no objection. Croft supports it but considers it partially redundant with Three-House Custody.
9. No Ready Passage — Drake's correlate to Krane's architecture. Croft supports it. Jones opposes it only if it blocks Research access — under Recontain it's compatible with Jones's position. Jones's acceptance: "Under Recontain, I'll take No Ready Passage in exchange for keeping Copy the Inscriptions."
10. Suppress the Location — The most contested Drake clause. Jones opposes it on grounds of logical inconsistency (three houses already know the location; the clause is unenforceable). Croft has ambivalence. Drake admits under cross-examination that it's more symbolic than operative. Suppress the Location is the first dropped clause.

**Slot 5 contest — the real negotiation:**

Four clauses are unanimous (slots 1-4). Slot 5 is fought over by: Withhold the Mechanism (Drake), No Ready Passage (Drake), Three-House Custody (Croft), Publish the Discovery (Jones).

The negotiation:

- Jones argues for Publish the Discovery paired with Withhold the Mechanism. "Publish the history; redact the manual. That's not dangerous — it's what any academic institution would do." Drake says: "If you're publishing, you're also telling the world a navigable temple exists with a mind-altering chamber. That's the dangerous part, not the technical mechanism."

- Croft argues for Three-House Custody over Three-Witness Rule because they serve different functions (Custody governs who holds the surviving record; Three-Witness Rule governs future access). Drake notes that Three-House Custody and No Private Keeping are both governance clauses covering the same concern. Drake is willing to sacrifice No Private Keeping (which is mostly about Drake's own behavior and can be honored informally) in exchange for keeping Withhold the Mechanism.

- The resolution: Jones proposes a swap. Drop Publish the Discovery (Jones's weakest championed clause under Recontain, since there's nothing to publish about Research access) in exchange for Withhold the Mechanism taking slot 5. Jones retains Copy the Inscriptions (the archival mission is intact) and can live without Publish the Discovery under Recontain. This trade satisfies Drake's containment platform (Withhold the Mechanism is the most important Drake clause when Destroy doesn't pass). Croft accepts because Three-House Custody, though not in the final five, is partially covered by Three-Witness Rule's requirement for multi-house participation.

**Final 5 clauses agreed:**
1. No Judgment Without Record (Jones)
2. Copy the Inscriptions (Jones)
3. Leave an Unmistakable Warning (Croft)
4. Three-Witness Rule (Croft)
5. Withhold the Mechanism (Drake)

**Dropped clauses and why:**
- Publish the Discovery: dropped by Jones in exchange for Withhold the Mechanism. The scholarly publication mission is partially satisfied by Copy the Inscriptions.
- Three-House Custody: dropped by Croft because Three-Witness Rule covers the multi-house requirement for future access; custody of surviving records is handled informally.
- No Ready Passage: Drake's second-choice clause, dropped because Recontain itself implies no ready passage; the major decision makes this clause somewhat redundant.
- No Private Keeping: dropped as partially redundant with Three-Witness Rule and accepted informally.
- Suppress the Location: dropped after Jones's logical inconsistency argument; Drake concedes.
- All remaining clauses: not seriously championed or not brought to the floor.

## Step 3 — Designer Assessment

### Did the deliberation produce a satisfying ending?

Yes, structurally — but the satisfaction skews heavily toward Croft. The negotiated outcome is Recontain (Croft's major decision) plus two Croft clauses. Croft entered the round table as the middle position and exited as the winner. This is plausible and has narrative logic (the moderate stewardship position often wins multi-party negotiations), but the emotional shape means Croft players have a triumphant arc and Jones players have a concession arc. Drake players have a grudging-acceptance arc. For 5-7 person teams, the Croft players will feel most fully realized; Jones players will need the Last Copyist's Oath payoff to feel their effort mattered.

The deliberation does feel like a choice the room made together rather than a default. The critical moment is Drake's concession in Round 3 (Drake vs. Croft): when Croft points out that the QRians' recontainment failed specifically because it was deceptive (the false staircase) and that a corrected Recontain with honest warning is structurally different, Drake has to genuinely reconsider. That pivot is earned by the evidence, not by social pressure. It is the deliberation's most satisfying single beat.

The slot-5 clause fight (Jones vs. Drake vs. Croft, three clauses competing for one spot) produces the sharpest negotiation moment. The Jones/Drake trade — drop Publish the Discovery, take Withhold the Mechanism — is a genuine compromise with real cost on both sides. This is the mechanic working as intended.

### Was the timeline reconstruction work worth the room time?

Partially. The timeline reconstruction confirms and deepens what Acts 1 and 2 established, but it does not reverse any major theory. Every house's broad arc reading was already correct from Acts 1 and 2. The specific payoff for reconstruction is the five-phase structure becoming legible as a single continuous arc rather than four disconnected fragments per house. The moment the room sees all twelve cards laid out in order — from Schoolmaster's pure blessing to Sefa's uncertain testimony — is emotionally significant. It is the only moment in Act 3 where the full QRian story is visible at once.

However, the reconstruction work produces two categories of outcome: (a) cards that confirm existing theories (majority), and (b) cards that produce new insight (minority). The new-insight cards are: Sefa's Testimony (revealed to Jones players for the first time that the QRians had land-killing alternatives), the Drain Order (revealed to Drake/Croft players as the Act 1 puzzle's explanation), and the Kitchen Complaint's spread-to-workers detail (revealed to Jones/Croft players who lacked Drake's fragment). These three cross-house payoffs justify the reconstruction work. The other nine fragments deepen rather than surprise.

Verdict: the timeline reconstruction earns its time if the host actively surfaces the three cross-house payoffs. Without host facilitation, the reconstruction risks feeling like group trivia (sorting cards you already broadly understood).

### Did the missing Vesh / Togom / Yenus material matter?

**Yenus:** Croft's explicit gap — the one named individual whose direct testimony was staged as inaccessible due to the Act 1 lockout consequence. No Yenus fragment exists in Act 3. In this simulation, Yenus's absence does not change the major decision or any clause pick. The room has enough evidence to reconstruct the Source's mechanism, timeline, and moral weight without Yenus's personal theory. However, Yenus's absence is felt as a narrative gap: Croft explicitly notes "the one QRian who hid his conclusions for a capable future reader." If the game promises that Yenus had something specific and inaccessible, not delivering even a partial resolution in Act 3 is a broken promise to the Croft players who registered the lockout as a real loss. The missing material is primarily atmosphere and character completeness, not decision-affecting. But Croft players will notice.

**Vesh (Act 2 botanical pollen hypothesis):** Vesh's documented transmission mechanism (airborne propagation) is partially confirmed by the Plain Warning's "the air below makes the mind wrong." The connection between Vesh's Act 2 botanical work and the Plain Warning's Act 3 confirmation is a genuine payoff — Jones players who tracked the pollen hypothesis will recognize it. But it does not change the deliberation; all three houses are already treating the Source as a proximity/ambient hazard.

**Togom (sealed bunker, Act 2):** Not resolved by any Act 3 fragment. Not missed in the deliberation. The Togom thread is currently a dead end.

**Summary:** If the unseeded Act 2→3 consequences were fully implemented, the most important missing payoff is Yenus. Adding a Yenus fragment (even a partial one — perhaps his theory was wrong, or incomplete, or confirmed what the room already suspected) would close the most prominent open narrative thread without changing any outcome. The missing Vesh/Togom material is atmosphere; Yenus is a structural promise that currently goes unfulfilled.

### Is the clause-card system well-calibrated?

**Strengths:** The five clauses that won the deliberation (No Judgment Without Record, Copy the Inscriptions, Leave an Unmistakable Warning, Three-Witness Rule, Withhold the Mechanism) each represent a distinct house's platform and a distinct dimension of the settlement. The package feels complete: documentation before action (Jones), knowledge preserved (Jones), legible warning (Croft), multi-house governance (Croft), mechanistic redaction (Drake). No single house dominates.

**Dead-weight clauses:** Suppress the Location is effectively dead weight. Its logical inconsistency under any outcome where three houses know the location is correctly identified by Jones in this simulation, and it is unlikely to survive deliberation in any playtest. The clause needs either a rewrite (e.g., "Agree not to publish the precise location in any academic or public document") or removal. As written, it invites a logic argument that derails the negotiation without producing interesting tension.

**Everyone-wants-it clauses:** No Judgment Without Record is unanimous. Its unanimity means it consumes a slot without generating any negotiation pressure. This is the least interesting clause from a game-design standpoint: it is obviously right under all three major decisions and costs nothing to agree on. Consider whether it should be baked into Act 3 as a standing rule (the room must document before any irreversible action) rather than a discrete clause slot.

**Good tension clauses:** Publish the Discovery vs. Withhold the Mechanism creates the sharpest single negotiation moment — they are not formally incompatible (Jones accepts Withhold as a paired safeguard) but they pull in opposite directions and force real concession. This pair is the clause system working at its best. No Ready Passage generates reasonable argument under Recontain (Drake wants it; Jones can accept it; Croft likes it). Three-Witness Rule vs. Three-House Custody is a Croft-internal competition that forces Croft to prioritize — good for the game, occasionally confusing for players who aren't sure what the difference is.

**Clauses no one champions:** No House Takes Credit is the weakest clause in the set. It has no dramatic payoff — the room is not arguing about credit at this stage — and its governance vectors (access: -1, governance: +2) are modest. It could be cut or replaced with a clause that generates genuine tension (e.g., a clause about what happens to the recovered cultural record specifically).

**Slot constraint (max 5):** The 5-slot limit creates exactly the right pressure in this simulation. With three unanimous clauses (No Judgment Without Record, Copy the Inscriptions, Leave an Unmistakable Warning) taking slots 1-3 before the fight begins, the remaining two slots carry all the negotiation weight. If the limit were 6, Publish the Discovery would likely survive without trade. If it were 4, one unanimous clause would have to be cut, which would force harder early choices. 5 is the right number for this clause set.

### Pacing

Phase 1 (per-house solo analysis): 25-35 minutes per house.

Act 3 timeline reconstruction: 15-20 minutes for a room that is actively engaged and has a designated organizer. Without a designated organizer, this can run 30+ minutes as twelve cards are read aloud by different people without coordination.

Round-table deliberation (Rounds 1-4): 25-40 minutes for a 15-24 person room. The major decision debate is the longest beat (15-20 minutes); clause negotiation is faster once the major decision is settled (10-15 minutes).

Clause selection and final recording: 5-10 minutes.

**Total Act 3 time estimate: 70-105 minutes.** This is appropriate for a final act. The upper end (105 minutes) is a risk for games that have already run long in Acts 1 and 2. The host brief should include a hard-stop guide: if the major decision is not settled within 20 minutes of opening deliberation, the host should intervene and call for a vote.

**Scalability:** The deliberation scales poorly above 18 players without designated house representatives. At 21-24 players, each house has 7-8 people, and consensus-building within the house before presenting to the room takes non-trivial time. Recommend the host brief instruct each house to designate a spokesperson before the round table opens.

### Emotional shape

The act has a clear emotional climax: the moment Drake concedes on Recontain. This is the pivot from three separate positions to a negotiable center. Everything after that is resolution, not climax.

The risk to the emotional shape is the timeline reconstruction. If the reconstruction runs long or feels like bookkeeping, it depletes the energy that should accumulate for the deliberation. The reconstruction needs to feel like revelation (seeing the full arc for the first time), not cataloguing (confirming what you already knew). The host brief should frame the reconstruction as "lay them out, read them aloud in sequence, experience the arc together" rather than "sort them correctly for verification."

The inscribing/recording beat at the end — where the settlement is formally committed — should be staged as a physical act: the host reads the major decision and each clause aloud while players hear what it means for their house. The house outcome texts from finale.ts are well-written for this moment. Jones's Vindicated text ("the truth survived in meaningful form") lands softly but correctly after a deliberation where Jones made real concessions. Croft's Vindicated text ("responsible steward: danger constrained, ownership shared, recklessness denied") is the most satisfying in this scenario. Drake's Vindicated text ("the threat was met decisively") is slightly off for a Recontain outcome — Drake may feel the consequence text is more apt for a Destroy outcome, and Drake players who know they settled for Recontain may register the dissonance. Consider adding a Drake Vindicated variant for the Recontain outcome specifically.

## Step 4 — Story of the Temple: Final

**Where each house landed:**

Drake entered Act 3 with a theory of institutional collapse and left with it confirmed and specified. The Source is documented, not mysterious. Drake's reading of the QRian arc — that the trap was built by people who understood what they were building — is accurate. Drake was unable to win the Destroy argument but succeeded in shaping the clause package toward containment and redaction. Drake's story is: "We came here for money and found something that makes money irrelevant. We closed it the right way." Drake never fully accounts for the financial temptation built into their house identity; the deliberation outcome sidesteps the question of whether Drake extracted anything.

Jones entered Act 3 as the house most at risk of forcing a bad outcome (Open for Research + extraction economy) and exited as the house that arguably saved the deliberation. Jones's concession — accepting Recontain in exchange for the archival clause package — prevented a two-against-one dynamic from forming and gave the settlement intellectual legitimacy. Jones's story is: "The archive survives. That is not what we came for, but it may be more important." Jones's theory of the Source shifted from "it can be managed with better protocols" to "the QRians tried exactly our protocols and failed — we succeeded not by managing access, but by managing what survives."

Croft entered Act 3 with the most complete and internally consistent arc and exited as the steward of the settlement. Croft's story is the cleanest arc in the game: from "blessing" (Schoolmaster) through "unable to stop" (Ennar) through "we hid it because we feared leaving nothing behind" (Copyists) to "we leave an unmistakable warning that is not a trap." Croft did not win the debate through cleverness or charisma — Croft won it by having the most complete evidence and the most precise argument. This is the game mechanic working: the house with the best-constructed fragment arc wins the major decision.

**How close the combined room theory came to canonical lore:**

The room reached approximately 85% of the canonical truth in lore-the-source.md.

What they got right: the four-phase arc (Gift → Obsession → Contagion → Seal) is fully reconstructed from the twelve fragments. The "Chaotic Order" concept — order that becomes chaos, logic that becomes madness — is described accurately via the Kitchen Complaint (geometric obsession), the Drain Order ("can no longer trust ourselves with a way back"), and Ennar's "becoming excellent at the cost of becoming unable to stop." The Source-as-radiation (not sentient, imposing compulsion on priorities rather than emotions) is correctly inferred by all three houses from the symptom descriptions. The QRian language evolving under the Source (the blocky geometric glyph system) is the single most beautiful lore detail, and it is flagged by Jones in Phase 1 via the Plain Warning's "the air below makes the mind wrong" / Learning Blocks' "new square symbols are easier to sort." Players who track the glyph font's cube-like design throughout Acts 1-2 will have the full picture. The slavery element of the construction (Krane's Act 2 compartment: "their elimination at completion was a kindness") is not surfaced in Act 3's twelve history cards, which means the full horror of how the temple was built remains available as an Act 2 revelation that does not need to be re-processed in Act 3's deliberation.

What they likely missed: the specific framing that the QRians believed the Source to be "the direct manifestation of the devil" (Phase 3 in lore) is not explicitly in any of the twelve history cards as written. Ennar's Priest-Scientist title gestures at the religious-scientific fusion, and the Learning Blocks' "keep them out of the sleeping rooms" implies sacred/profane contamination logic, but the explicit "devil" framing does not emerge. The "went extinct" conclusion — that QRian civilization did not survive in diminished form — is also not in the twelve fragments. The room will infer extinction from the absence of survivors, but may leave open the question of whether some QRians fled and survived elsewhere. For a game called "Temple of the QRians," leaving the extinction question open is probably the right creative choice.
