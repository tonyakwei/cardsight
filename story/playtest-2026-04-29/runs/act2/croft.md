# Croft Company — Act 2 Playtest

## Carry-forward state (Act 1 → Act 2)

Entering Act 2 with a specific wound: Mission 1 (Grappling Rigs) was failed in Act 1 due to the #4/#6 ordering ambiguity — a puzzle with genuinely ambiguous card text, not a reading failure. The consequence is Mission 5 (High Ledge) is locked. We also carry forward a working hypothesis that the QRians were systematic pedagogical builders who encoded knowledge into physical objects, that the temple was designed to seal and trap, and that at least one QRian died here writing a final message to their child. The most unsettled question from Act 1: why does the temple trap its own builders? Act 2 should answer or deepen this.

### Mission 1: The Reckoning Floor

**Trade list:**
- Need from Drake: Five Calculation Tablet
- Need from Jones: Seven Calculation Tablet, Nine Calculation Tablet
- Offering to Drake in return: Apothecary Note Torn (Batch C), Drevu's Compartment Tile Slot 3, Red Wall Tile 3, Bark Label (Station 5)
- Offering to Jones in return: Painted Clay Shelf-Label (Shelves 3+4), Amber Wall Tile 3, Vesh's Compartment Tile Slot 3, Burial-Rite Fragment Charred, Sefa Riddle-Tablet II

**Working:**

The mechanic: four digit-classes (3, 5, 7, 9) each produce one letter. Each digit-class has its own calculation tablet that tells players which pebbles to trace and in what order, and the traced path forms a QRian super-glyph. Four super-glyphs → four letters → one English word.

Croft holds the Three Calculation Tablet, which handles the first letter. The tablet text confirms the mechanic clearly: "Find every 3 on the floor record bearing a sequence number. Trace them in order. Their path is the first letter of the answer." The digit-class order (3, 5, 7, 9) gives positions 1, 2, 3, 4 in the final word.

The puzzle's content question — "What were the QRians measuring?" — paired with the story blurb ("They were measuring how much time they had. The floor froze where the answer ran out") points directly at the answer. The floor is a time-measurement calculation. The QRians were tracking a deadline.

Candidate answer: **TIME**

This is four letters. The digit-classes 3-5-7-9 map to positions 1-2-3-4 of the word. T-I-M-E. Thematically exact — the floor records a count that ran out. The word is also accessible enough that a real team will feel the "aha" without it being handed to them. No other four-letter word describing what the QRians were calculating has the same combination of thematic fit and simplicity.

**Final answer:** TIME
**Confidence:** medium — the mechanics are clear and the thematic pointer is strong, but without the other three tablets (which provide the actual glyph-tracing paths) I can't verify the letter derivations. The answer is inferred from context rather than derived from mechanic execution.
**Difficulty:** 3
**Time-to-solve guess:** 15–25 minutes for a 5-7 person team; the mechanic requires trading four cards before anything is solvable, and then requires a physical tracing exercise that doesn't translate cleanly to verbal communication.
**Solving mode:** guessed (context-driven inference; real mechanic execution requires physical tablet tracing I cannot perform without the other cards)
**Trade need:** Three out of four tablets are with other houses (Drake: Five; Jones: Seven and Nine). This is the most trade-heavy mission in the act for Croft — cannot start solving without all four.
**Sticking points:**

1. The tracing mechanic ("trace every 3-pebble in sequence-number order, the path draws a letter") is described but the actual pebble floor is a printed physical artifact — not included in this briefing. Players must execute this on a physical floor sheet. In the playtest, this step cannot be simulated analytically; the glyph-tracing is the puzzle. This makes the mission essentially unverifiable in a text-based playtest.

2. The "digit-class" framing (why 3, 5, 7, 9 — the odd numbers above 1) is not explained to players. Players will need to infer or be told that these four digit-classes are the relevant ones. If players don't know there are exactly four, they might look for digit-classes 1, 2, 4, 6, 8 as well, or not know to stop at four letters.

3. The Three Calculation Tablet says it produces "the first letter." But which of 3/5/7/9 is "first"? The tablet for digit-class 3 is the one Croft holds, and it says "first letter." This seems to order them 3→5→7→9 giving letters 1→2→3→4. This ordering (ascending) is the most natural but should be confirmed by whether the other tablets say "second," "third," "fourth." If they don't, players may try all orderings.

**Felt earned?:** partial — the answer TIME lands well thematically, and the four-part tracing mechanic is architecturally satisfying. But this mission cannot actually be solved without physical floor sheets. It's the most dependent on a physical artifact that the briefing does not provide.

**Designer flags:**

- The physical floor sheet is load-bearing and must be present for this puzzle to function at all. In a real playtest this is fine; in any kind of remote or paper-only playtest, the puzzle is unresolvable.
- Confirm that each Calculation Tablet explicitly names its position (first, second, third, fourth letter) — if only the Three Tablet does this, players holding the other tablets may not know the ordering.
- The puzzle description says "Four super-glyphs spell a single English word" — this is appropriately informative. Players will know to assemble a four-letter word, which constrains the search.
- Strong design: making Croft hold only ONE of the four tablets (while Jones holds TWO) creates asymmetric trade dependency. Croft needs to negotiate with two houses. This forces engagement. Good calibration.

### Mission 2: The Sighting Wall

**Trade list:**
- Need from Drake: Lens-Label (Clusters 3+4: HEAT+LIE, SHADOW+SUN)
- Need from Jones: Lens-Label (Cluster 5: DAY+DEATH)
- Offering to Drake in return: (same pool as Mission 1 — Apothecary Note, Drevu's tile, Red Wall Tile 3, Bark Label)
- Offering to Jones in return: (same pool — Painted Clay Shelf-Label, Amber Wall Tile 3, Vesh's tile, Burial-Rite Fragment, Sefa Riddle-Tablet II)

**Note on self-destruct:** The Lens-Label cards have a 60-second timer. This is a genuine urgency mechanic — the team must scan the cards and transcribe the label words immediately. In a real room, this means whoever scans the card has 60 seconds to read and announce the two label words before they vanish. Teams should designate a writer before scanning.

**Working:**

Each cluster has two label-words. The task is to name the phenomenon being observed. The story frames this as the QRians measuring whether their location was "special" — astronomical/celestial observation. The memory context explicitly identifies these as "liminal celestial phenomena."

Reading each pair as a phenomenological description:

**Cluster 1 (Croft holds): NIGHT + SURRENDER**
When night surrenders, what happens? Night surrenders to day. The phenomenon of night yielding = DAWN. Alternatively, "surrender" in a celestial context could mean eclipse (night surrenders to unexpected darkness midday? No, that's the opposite). The most direct reading: night giving way = DAWN.

**Cluster 2 (Croft holds): DARK + THRONE**
Darkness on the throne — darkness at its highest position / peak authority. This is MIDNIGHT (when darkness reigns fully). Or: TOTALITY (in an eclipse, when the dark is complete). Given the QRians are measuring liminal phenomena, MIDNIGHT fits cleanly — the turning point of night.

**Cluster 3 (Drake holds): HEAT + LIE**
Heat that deceives / lies / is not what it appears. This is MIRAGE — the phenomenon of heat creating optical illusions. Clean and obvious once stated.

**Cluster 4 (Drake holds): SHADOW + SUN**
The sun in shadow — or the shadow of the sun falling on something. ECLIPSE is the canonical celestial phenomenon where shadow and sun meet. Alternatively: TRANSIT (a body transiting the sun's face), but ECLIPSE is more universal and fits the two-word construction perfectly.

**Cluster 5 (Jones holds): DAY + DEATH**
Day's death = DUSK or SUNSET. "Death of day" is sunset/dusk. SUNSET is the more specific term; DUSK is the phenomenal period. Both work. SUNSET is likely the intended answer given its directness.

**Final answer:** DAWN MIDNIGHT MIRAGE ECLIPSE SUNSET
**Confidence:** medium-high — the word-pair logic is consistent and each pairing has a clear dominant reading. The risk is that the designers had specific QRian names or English terms in mind (e.g., "TWILIGHT" instead of "DUSK/SUNSET") and the variations are narrow but real.
**Difficulty:** 2
**Time-to-solve guess:** 8–14 minutes (after trading), plus trading time
**Solving mode:** understood-mechanic — the pairing logic is clear, the word readings are natural
**Trade need:** Croft holds Clusters 1+2 (2 of 5 label-pairs), needs Clusters 3+4 from Drake and Cluster 5 from Jones
**Sticking points:**

1. The self-destruct timer (60 seconds) makes this mission time-pressured in a way that depends on coordination. The team scanning the cards must have a designated writer and must be ready before scanning. If multiple people scan different cards, you have 60 seconds per card but you need to pool the information before it's gone. This is good tension if the room is organized; it's chaotic noise if the team doesn't coordinate first.

2. NIGHT+SURRENDER → DAWN vs. TWILIGHT. Both work. If the answer validation requires an exact string, this pair is most likely to produce wrong answers from teams who think "TWILIGHT" or "DUSK" (as the reverse, night beginning rather than ending).

3. DARK+THRONE → MIDNIGHT is clean but a team might go to TOTALITY (from eclipse) or NADIR (astronomical term for the bottom of the sky arc). MIDNIGHT is the most accessible answer.

4. No puzzle here tells players that the phenomenon names should be common English words rather than technical astronomical terms. A team with an astronomer might go: ZENITH, NADIR, UMBRA, TRANSIT, SYZYGY. These are likely wrong. The framing "Name each phenomenon" should be paired with an instruction that the answer is a plain English word or short phrase.

**Felt earned?:** yes — the word-pair mechanic is elegant and the phenomena are appropriately evocative. The 60-second self-destruct adds genuine urgency that's thematically fitting (label flaking apart in the air). This is the most mechanically satisfying mission in the act for Croft.

**Designer flags:**

- The self-destruct mechanic on these specific cards is the best design choice in the Croft packet. It's narratively justified (labels flake), mechanically interesting (forces coordination), and teaches players that writing things down is mandatory. This should be held up as a model.
- **Critical flag:** The answer validation needs to accept answer variants. DAWN/SUNRISE, MIDNIGHT/TOTALITY, MIRAGE/HALLUCINATION, ECLIPSE/TRANSIT, DUSK/SUNSET — there are at least two plausible answers for most clusters. The answer template should either: (a) accept all reasonable variants, or (b) give players a tiebreaker in the card text. As written, the card text gives the two label words with no disambiguating context.
- The cross-house structure (Croft 2, Drake 2, Jones 1) is well-balanced — everyone contributes, no single house dominates. Croft has the most at two pairs, which matches Croft's observational/measurement theme.
- Consider: do the five phenomena form a progression or pattern when read in order? DAWN → MIDNIGHT → MIRAGE → ECLIPSE → SUNSET reads as a day cycle with some celestial events. If there's a meta-puzzle or a meaning to the sequence, it's not visible in the briefing.

### Mission 3: Krane's Compartment

**Trade list:**
- Need from Drake: Krane's Compartment Tile, Slot 1 (clear / ebb)
- Need from Jones: Krane's Compartment Tile, Slot 2 (gather / gather)
- Offering to Drake in return: same card pool as above
- Offering to Jones in return: same card pool as above

**Working:**

The full procedure reconstructed after trading:

**Procedure I:** clear (step 1) → gather (step 2) → darken (step 3) → **?**
**Procedure II:** ebb (step 1) → gather (step 2) → wave (step 3) → **?**

Procedure I analysis: clear → gather → darken
This describes sky conditions: a clear sky → clouds gather → sky darkens. The next step is RAIN or STORM. The sequence maps to a weather progression. STORM is more complete (covers the event not just the precipitation); RAIN is more specific and physical. Given the QRian tendency to name the state rather than the process, STORM seems right.

Alternative reading: clear → gather → darken → HIDE (as in, the thing that was visible gets hidden by what gathered and darkened). But this is a stretch.

Procedure II analysis: ebb → gather → wave
This describes water: water ebbs (pulls back) → gathers (accumulates as a swell) → waves (rises to peak). The next step is BREAK or CRASH or FLOOD. BREAK is the most accurate description of what a wave does at its end. CRASH is more dramatic. FLOOD is what happens if the wave doesn't retreat — and given the Act 1 context (the temple flooded), FLOOD carries thematic weight here.

Cross-checking with the QRian context: Krane built the temple and left a working note in a sealed compartment. These procedures may describe natural phenomena the QRians were modeling or controlling. A sky-storm procedure and a water-tidal procedure both relate to large-scale environmental processes. If the compartment contains Krane's theory about what the temple was designed to do — manage or trigger a flood — then FLOOD as the answer for Procedure II is narratively resonant.

**Final answer:** STORM FLOOD
**Confidence:** medium — STORM is high confidence; FLOOD vs. BREAK is the harder call. BREAK is more technically correct for a wave cycle; FLOOD is more thematically loaded given the temple context.
**Difficulty:** 3
**Time-to-solve guess:** 12–20 minutes (the inference step for step 4 is the puzzle; trading is also required)
**Solving mode:** understood-mechanic — the progression logic is clear once all tiles are pooled
**Trade need:** Slots 1 (Drake) and 2 (Jones) are required; Croft only holds Slot 3
**Sticking points:**

1. The "infer step 4" requirement is the core puzzle, and it's genuinely open-ended. The procedures are natural-language progressions, not mathematical sequences — there are defensible multiple completions. A real team will debate STORM vs RAIN vs POUR and BREAK vs CRASH vs FLOOD vs SURGE.

2. "Step 4 is the passcode" framing is good design: players know they're solving for a single word per procedure. But without a verification mechanism (does the compartment "open" in some physical way?), teams in a real room may not know if their answer is correct. The submit-an-answer interface handles this digitally, but the narrative framing should make clear that the word is the passcode (it does — "The compartment opens").

3. Procedure II: ebb → gather → wave. "Gather" appears in both procedures at step 2. This could confuse players who assume the two procedures must be independent (different words at every step). In fact they share the middle step — which is interesting design (both phenomena converge at "gathering") but may require a moment of re-reading to accept.

**Felt earned?:** yes — the progression logic is satisfying and the inference feels earned rather than arbitrary. The two procedures feel distinct (sky vs. water) and the shared "gather" middle step is an elegant detail. The compartment reveal (Krane's working note) is a good narrative payoff.

**Designer flags:**

- Multiple valid completions for both procedures. The answer validation must accept: STORM/RAIN/POUR/BURST for Proc I, and BREAK/CRASH/FLOOD/SURGE for Proc II. At minimum, two answers each. As written, a team could solve this correctly with RAIN BREAK and be marked wrong if the canonical answer is STORM FLOOD.
- The shared "gather" at step 2 for both procedures is worth calling out explicitly in the puzzle description — otherwise teams may assume it's a transcription error and try to find different words.
- Krane's identity (he "built the temple") was not established in Act 1. This is the first named QRian character. The compartment is "his." Players will want to know who Krane was and why his compartment is significant. A one-sentence character note would help: "Krane: chief architect of the Green Department, the last to seal the upper passages."
- The mechanics here interact with the broader mythology: Krane left a working note that encodes weather/water procedures. If these procedures describe the mechanisms the temple uses (flooding triggered by a storm, water managed by tide cycles), then Krane's compartment is load-bearing for Act 3 understanding. Is it? If yes, make that connection explicit in the story blurb.

**[STORY LOG ENTRY 3: Theory after Mission 3]**

Krane was the architect. He left procedures for STORM and FLOOD — not as puzzles, but as working notes. The QRians were modeling or building systems to generate or respond to flooding. This reframes Act 1's flood: the water rising wasn't a malfunction or a visitor triggering something accidental. Krane's compartment suggests this was planned — a tidal mechanism, a storm cycle, something designed. The QRians built the flood. The shift is beginning here — "obsessive builders" still fits, but the obsession is starting to look less like scholarship and more like compulsion. Krane sealed his compartment. ### Mission 4: The Wall of Repetitions, Purple

**Trade list:**
- Need from Drake: Purple Wall Tile 2
- Need from Jones: Purple Wall Tile 3
- Offering: same cards as before

**Working:**

The mechanic: three 21-character strings (one per tile), majority-vote per position to reconstruct the original inscription.

Croft holds Tile 1: `UFTIKWESQOWVYREJLPZEX`

Tile 2 is with Drake, Tile 3 is with Jones. Without those strings I cannot run the reconstruction. However, the puzzle structure allows me to analyze what I can and document the mechanic clearly.

Character count: `UFTIKWESQOWVYREJLPZEX` = 21 characters. Confirmed — matches the stated 21-character inscription length.

The majority-vote mechanic: for each of the 21 positions, I look at the three tiles' characters and identify which letter appears twice (the original) vs. once (the drift). This produces a clean 21-character original inscription.

**What the inscription likely says:** The purple section is described as "the hinge of the confession, wisdom turning to obsession slowly enough to write down." The Wall of Repetitions (all three colors combined) represents the QRians repeatedly rewriting the same phrase as they drift toward obsession. The purple section is specifically the turning point — the moment wisdom tips into compulsion.

Without Tiles 2 and 3 I cannot derive the actual letters. The answer format is a 21-character phrase. Based on the established thematic arc, the inscription likely describes the relationship between the QRians and the Source: something they first understood clearly, then began repeating compulsively.

**Attempted partial reconstruction using what I have:**
Tile 1: `U F T I K W E S Q O W V Y R E J L P Z E X`

Without other tiles, every character on Tile 1 could be either original or drift. I cannot identify which without the comparison. No partial derivation is possible — the mechanic requires all three tiles.

**Final answer:** CANNOT DERIVE — requires Purple Wall Tiles 2 and 3 (held by Drake and Jones). The mechanic is understood; the execution is impossible without the full card set.
**Confidence:** N/A (mechanic understood, answer underivable without other tiles)
**Difficulty:** 3 (the mechanic is clear once all three tiles are in hand; the reconstruction is systematic, not creative)
**Time-to-solve guess:** 10–15 minutes once all three tiles are gathered (pure position-by-position comparison). Trading time adds to this.
**Solving mode:** understood-mechanic — but could not execute without all three cards
**Trade need:** Both other tiles required (Drake: Tile 2, Jones: Tile 3)
**Sticking points:**

1. This puzzle cannot be started at all until all three tiles are in hand. Unlike Mission 1 where Croft's tile explains the mechanic, here Croft's tile provides only one-third of the data. The puzzle is entirely blocked on trading.

2. The majority-vote mechanic is well-designed for a group: all three players (one from each house, holding one tile each) can stand together and call out characters position by position. Natural collaborative mechanic. However it requires coordination, either physically (all three people at one table) or verbally (reading strings aloud and cross-referencing). In a real room this is fun; in a written playtest it's unexecutable.

3. The cross-house meta-connection is compelling: "Jones is reading the Amber wall; Drake is reading the Red wall. When the host calls it, combine all three answers to read the full three-line inscription." This means the Wall of Repetitions is a full-act payoff — all three houses' answers combine. This is excellent design. The host calling it creates a moment of shared revelation.

**Felt earned?:** Partially — the mechanic is elegant and the collaborative resolution (all three houses combine at the end) is the strongest inter-house payoff in the game. But this mission is entirely dependent on the other two houses' tiles and cannot be experienced in isolation at all. The single-tile preview (Croft's Tile 1) gives no usable information and exists only as a trading chip.

**Designer flags:**

- **Most important flag in the act:** The Wall of Repetitions missions (Purple/Amber/Red) require all three houses to have completed their wall missions before the meta-answer is knowable. This creates a synchronization problem: if one house is stuck or trading slowly, none of the three houses can get the combined payoff. In a real room, this could mean three houses all solve their individual walls but never get to combine them if act-time runs out. Consider whether the host's "call it" moment needs a buffer or a fallback reveal.
- The 21-character reconstruction mechanic is sound and the drift/majority-vote framing is elegant. But players need to know to do this column-by-column — a worked example with three short strings might help players understand "two-beats-one" before they run it on the full 21-character set.
- The purple section being "the hinge of the confession, wisdom turning to obsession" is the most important narrative beat in Act 2. The inscription content must carry this weight. What does it actually say? If it says something like "WE DO WHAT IT ASKS OF US NOW" or "IT WILL NOT STOP AND NEITHER WILL WE," that's the moment the Source mythology becomes undeniable. If it says something vague, the story beat will not land.
- Confirm: does the host's combined three-wall inscription form a coherent three-line statement? If yes, that statement is the narrative climax of Act 2 and needs to be strong. The designer should verify the full three-line combination before finalizing the tile drift values.

**[STORY LOG ENTRY 4: Theory after Mission 4]**

The Wall of Repetitions is where the QRians recorded their own dissolution. The purple section — "wisdom turning to obsession slowly enough to write down" — means the people in this room were aware it was happening. They were not suddenly corrupted; they watched themselves change, wrote it down, kept writing it, kept changing. This is the clearest evidence yet that something external was influencing them. You do not write an observation compulsively unless the compulsion is being imposed. The wall is simultaneously a record and a symptom. The Source is not just an object in the temple — it is something that acted on the people who built this place, and they knew it, and they could not stop. The shift from "institutional" to "environmental" — something is wrong with these people — happened here, in this mission, at the phrase "wisdom turning to obsession slowly enough to write down."

### Mission 5: The High Ledge

**MISSION LOCKED — DID NOT ATTEMPT**

**What the mission would have required:**
- The Grappling Rigs from Act 1 (the crew item that was not secured due to Mission 1 failure)
- Yenus Riddle-Tablet I (held by Croft — one of Croft's clue cards in the packet)
- Yenus Riddle-Tablet II (held by Drake — would require a single-card trade)

**The riddle Croft holds (Yenus Riddle-Tablet I):** *"The gift you always have, at all times."* This is a riddle whose answer is something universally possessed and temporally constant. The answer is almost certainly PRESENT (you always have the present moment, at all times) — or TIME (you always have time with you). Given "TIME" is likely the answer to Mission 1 (Reckoning Floor), there may be a deliberate thematic echo. PRESENT also works as a riddle answer: you always have the present; it's always the present; the word means both "now" and "a gift."

Best guess for Riddle-Tablet I answer: PRESENT

Without Drake's Riddle-Tablet II, I don't know the second answer. The mission would have been two words (two riddle answers) separated by a space.

**How losing this mission felt:**

The lockout is the right consequence for the right wrong. The grappling rigs in Act 1 were Croft's physical equipment — the tools of our identity as technical climbers. We failed to secure our own tools. The consequence is that we can't climb. This has clean logical causality: the narrative reason for the lockout matches who Croft is (we climb things; we lost our climbing gear; we can't climb this ledge).

The twelve-foot smooth rock ledge is specifically calibrated: too high to jump, too smooth to climb without gear. The lockout isn't "a door is locked." It's a physical limitation — our means of reaching the ledge are gone. That feels more like consequence than punishment.

What stings: Croft holds Yenus Riddle-Tablet I — the first riddle, which we can read and partially solve, but cannot use. Holding a key to a door we can't reach is the right kind of frustrating. It makes the lockout feel lived-in rather than administrative.

What's missing: there's no in-game moment where another player witnesses the lockout alongside us. In a real room, if a member of Croft goes to the ledge and says "I can't reach it — we don't have the rigs," that's a social moment. The other players respond. The lockout lands differently in that moment than it does reading a brief. The design is correct; the realization must happen at the table.

**Final answer:** MISSION LOCKED — DID NOT ATTEMPT
**Confidence:** locked
**Difficulty:** locked
**Solving mode:** locked
**Trade need:** Would have needed Yenus Riddle-Tablet II from Drake (one card, single trade — low cost, which makes the inaccessibility of the ledge itself the binding constraint, not trading friction)

## Trade summary

**What Croft needed (per mission):**
- M1: Drake (Five Calculation Tablet) + Jones (Seven Calculation Tablet, Nine Calculation Tablet) — 3 cards from 2 houses
- M2: Drake (Lens-Label Clusters 3+4) + Jones (Lens-Label Cluster 5) — 2 cards from 2 houses (but these are one card per cluster, so 2 total)
- M3: Drake (Krane's Tile Slot 1) + Jones (Krane's Tile Slot 2) — 2 cards from 2 houses
- M4: Drake (Purple Wall Tile 2) + Jones (Purple Wall Tile 3) — 2 cards from 2 houses

**What Croft offered in exchange:**
- To Drake: Apothecary Note Torn (Batch C), Drevu's Compartment Tile Slot 3, Red Wall Tile 3, Bark Label Station 5: FLOWER+SPIRIT — 4 cards
- To Jones: Painted Clay Shelf-Label (Shelves 3+4), Amber Wall Tile 3, Vesh's Compartment Tile Slot 3, Burial-Rite Fragment Charred (openers), Sefa Riddle-Tablet II — 5 cards

**Totals:**
- Croft needs to receive: 9 cards (4 from Drake, 5 from Jones — counting by item, not by physical card)
- Croft has available to give: 9 cards (4 to Drake, 5 to Jones)

**Plausibility check:**

The trading balance looks good on paper — Croft is both a significant receiver and a significant giver. The critical question is whether Drake and Jones actually need what Croft has, and whether they're willing to part with what Croft needs.

From Croft's perspective: the offer to Drake (4 cards) in exchange for what Croft needs from Drake (4 items across multiple missions) is roughly balanced. The offer to Jones (5 cards) for what Croft needs from Jones (5 items) is also balanced. The trades are not one-sided.

**Risk points:**

1. The Lens-Label cards (Mission 2) have a 60-second self-destruct. This means the trade with Drake (Clusters 3+4) and Jones (Cluster 5) must happen before scanning — you negotiate the trade first, then simultaneously scan and transcribe. If Drake or Jones scans their labels before the trade, the information is time-limited (60 seconds to communicate). Teams should establish a "Mission 2 coordination moment" where all three houses are ready before any label cards are scanned.

2. Croft holds cards that Drake needs for multiple missions (Drake's Drevu's Compartment, Drake's Wall of Repetitions Red, Drake's Reagent Alcove). This gives Croft real trading leverage over Drake. Drake cannot complete these missions without Croft's cards. This asymmetry works in Croft's favor.

3. Similarly, Croft holds cards Jones needs for five missions. Croft is the largest single provider to Jones in this act. Jones should be motivated to deal.

4. One potential sticking point: both Croft-needs-from-Jones cards are for the same missions where Jones needs Croft's cards. The Amber Wall Tile 3 / Wall of Repetitions missions are parallel structures — all three houses need one wall tile from each other. This means the Wall of Repetitions trade is a natural simultaneous three-way exchange that should happen early (all three houses benefit immediately).

5. **Would this negotiation close in a real room?** Yes, with good confidence. The exchange is balanced and mutual dependencies are obvious. The only friction point is the self-destruct urgency on Mission 2 — that specific sub-trade requires pre-coordination. A real host might need to prompt teams to coordinate before anyone scans their Lens-Label cards.

6. **Asymmetry concern:** Croft provides 5 items to Jones (Jones's largest supplier) while only needing 4 items in total from Jones across four missions. If Jones also needs things from Drake that Drake is reluctant to give up, Jones could be stuck in a multi-house bottleneck. Croft's ability to complete all four missions is partly dependent on Jones being able to trade freely. This second-order dependency is worth the designer noting.

## End-of-act theory of the temple

The Temple of the QRians is a documentation system built by people who watched themselves change and kept writing it down. The Act 1 theory — systematic pedagogical builders, a school for future visitors — is partially right and partially wrong. The QRians were systematic and pedagogical, but the curriculum was not about transmitting knowledge to students: it was about preserving a record for whoever came after, after the QRians themselves could no longer hold the information clearly.

The Source is environmental — something present in or around the temple that influences cognition and behavior. The Wall of Repetitions is the clearest evidence: the QRians wrote the same phrase with incremental drift, aware they were losing fidelity but unable to stop. This is not scholarship. This is compulsion being documented by the people experiencing it. The Source acts on people who spend extended time in the temple. The QRians were the first long-term residents.

The temple seals because the QRians built the sealing mechanism deliberately. The Reckoning Floor shows a countdown; the Sighting Wall shows astronomical observation of liminal transitions; the sealing is tied to a specific moment or alignment. The QRians built the flood (Act 1) and the sealing (Act 2) as mechanisms — not defenses against intruders but containment structures for what was inside. They were keeping the Source in, or they were keeping people from reaching the Source before the right conditions were met.

The named individuals (Krane, Yenus) are not abstract temple-builders. They made personal choices: Krane sealed his own working notes and left procedures for flood and tidal mechanics; Yenus hid his personal theory at twelve feet above floor level, above where floodwater could reach, addressed to someone competent enough to climb. These people knew what was happening and chose to leave records rather than warnings. That is either confidence or resignation.

The institutional-to-environmental shift — the point where "obsessive builders" becomes "something is wrong with these people" — landed at Mission 4 (Wall of Repetitions), specifically the phrase "wisdom turning to obsession slowly enough to write down." By Act 2 end, Croft understands the QRians were not just obsessive: they were being made obsessive, they knew it, and they documented it rather than fled. The question for Act 3 is why they stayed.

## Designer summary (most important)

### Hardest puzzles

1. **Mission 1 (Reckoning Floor)** — hardest in terms of dependency. Three of four required cards are with other houses, AND the puzzle requires a physical floor artifact not present in the briefing. Uncompletable without both trading and the physical material. If the physical floor sheet is unclear or difficult to trace, this mission will be the primary bottleneck.

2. **Mission 3 (Krane's Compartment)** — the inference step (step 4) is genuinely open-ended. Multiple defensible answers for both procedures. Answer validation must be generous. Teams will argue RAIN vs STORM and BREAK vs FLOOD; if the validation is narrow, this mission will cause wrong-answer frustration.

3. **Mission 4 (Wall of Repetitions, Purple)** — impossible to begin until all three tiles are pooled, and the reconstruction is systematic rather than creative once trading is done. Difficulty is entirely in trade-coordination. Once all three tiles are at one table, solving is mechanical.

### Puzzles with mechanic/clue/answer problems

- **Mission 1:** The physical floor artifact is load-bearing and not replicated in any card text. If the floor sheet is unclear, ambiguous, or physically degraded during play, the mission fails. Also: the per-tablet positional ordering (does each tablet explicitly name its position in the word?) needs confirmation.

- **Mission 2 (Sighting Wall):** Multiple valid English words for each cluster. NIGHT+SURRENDER = DAWN or TWILIGHT or DUSK (depending on framing direction). DARK+THRONE = MIDNIGHT or TOTALITY. Answer validation must be generous or the mission will have a high false-negative rate. Also: no instruction tells players the answers should be common English words rather than technical astronomical terms.

- **Mission 3 (Krane's Compartment):** Both procedures have multiple valid completions. STORM/RAIN/POUR for Proc I; BREAK/CRASH/FLOOD/SURGE for Proc II. Current canonical answers unclear. This is the highest risk for wrong-answer frustration.

- **Mission 4:** The mission brief says "at every position, the letter that appears on two tiles is the truth; the lone outlier is drift" — this is clear. But a 21-character string is long to compare position-by-position without a grid. Players should have a physical or digital grid template (three rows, 21 columns) to fill in. Running this purely by reading strings aloud is error-prone.

- **Mission 5 (lockout):** No mechanic issue — correctly locked. The riddle on Croft's Tablet I ("the gift you always have, at all times") is solvable as PRESENT and is elegantly constructed. The second riddle is unseen, but the single-card trade requirement (Tablet II from Drake) suggests it would have been a low-friction trade. The mission design is clean; the lockout consequence is clean.

### Puzzles that worked well

- **Mission 2 (Sighting Wall):** The self-destruct mechanic on the lens-labels is the single best individual card design in the act. Narratively justified, mechanically interesting, thematically on-point (the QRians' records are decaying), and it teaches players the write-things-down behavior. Hold this up as the model for "what a self-destruct card should do."

- **Mission 3 (Krane's Compartment):** The shared "gather" at step 2 of both procedures is elegant — both sky-storm and tidal cycles have a gathering phase. This detail rewards close reading and doesn't confuse once noticed. The two-procedure structure with a single blank per procedure is clean scope.

- **Mission 5 (lockout framing):** "Twelve feet of sheer rock above the chamber floor — too high to jump, too smooth to climb." The physical description of why the lockout is absolute is exactly right. It's not administrative ("this mission is unavailable"); it's environmental ("the ledge is there, you can see it, you cannot reach it"). Croft holding Riddle-Tablet I while locked out of the ledge is the correct emotional note — we have one key to a door we can't open.

- **The Wall of Repetitions meta-structure:** Three houses each solve one color-section, then the host combines all three. This is the best large-scale inter-house design in the act. All three houses contribute to a single payoff. If the combined inscription is strong, this will be the best moment of Act 2.

### Story coherence

The Source mythology lands in Act 2, specifically at Mission 4. The phrase "wisdom turning to obsession slowly enough to write down" is the inflection point — it is the first moment where the QRians' behavior is described as an observed deterioration rather than a choice. Before Mission 4, the QRians seem purposeful (counting time, watching the sky, archiving procedures). At Mission 4, they seem driven.

The institutional → environmental shift happens at the right moment in the act (Mission 4 of 5). The five missions form a good escalating arc: measurement (M1) → observation (M2) → archival procedure (M3) → psychological deterioration (M4) → personal theory hidden from the flood (M5 — or its absence, in Croft's case).

**Gaps:**

- The Source is never named or described directly in the Croft packet. The Act 2 brief says "there's something coming" and "Croft's instinct: read what they were measuring." The connection between "what they were measuring" and "the Source" is left to player inference. This is appropriate — the QRians didn't name it either — but a fragment of explicit Source-language would help players make the connection. The Krane compartment is the best opportunity: his sealed working note could contain the phrase "what we built this for."

- Yenus's inaccessible theory (Mission 5) is the most tantalizing gap. The story acknowledges Yenus expected future visitors and hid his conclusions for them. Not reaching his ledge means Croft goes into Act 3 with the most important individual QRian's theory unread. This is good narrative design — the lockout has story consequences, not just mechanical ones.

- The named QRians (Krane, Yenus) are introduced without backstory. Who are they relative to each other? Knowing that Krane was the architect and Yenus was the theorist (or whatever their roles were) would help players form a mental model of QRian society.

### Pacing

**Time pressure: real but unevenly distributed.** The host narration ("the temple is sealing itself — you can feel it in your teeth") creates ambient urgency. The Lens-Label self-destruct (Mission 2) is the only mechanically enforced time pressure. Everything else is narrative. This creates uneven pacing: Mission 2 feels urgent; Missions 1, 3, 4 feel measured; Mission 5 (lockout) has no time component at all.

For a real team:
- M1 (Reckoning Floor): 15–25 min after trading; will be slow due to physical floor tracing
- M2 (Sighting Wall): 8–14 min after trading; self-destruct creates real urgency spike
- M3 (Krane's Compartment): 12–20 min after trading; inference debate adds time
- M4 (Wall of Repetitions): 10–15 min after trading; systematic once tiles are pooled
- M5: Locked — 0 time, immediate emotional impact on reading

Total estimated time (4 active missions): 45–74 minutes. Primary variables: physical floor tracing (M1) and inference debate (M3). If both are fast, the act is 45 minutes. If both stall, 75+.

**Where teams will stall:** Mission 1 (trading + physical tracing) and Mission 3 (inference debate). Mission 4 is the fastest solve once trading is done.

**Where trading adds friction:** Every mission requires cross-house cards. The trading volume is high — Croft needs 9 items from other houses and provides 9 to other houses. In a room of 15-24 players, this means significant cross-table movement during the trading phase. If houses don't coordinate early, Croft may find itself waiting on cards before it can start any mission. Consider whether some missions can be partially worked before all cards arrive (M3 and M4 can: Croft can pre-analyze their own tile/slot while waiting for the others).

### Trading mechanic feedback

**Well-calibrated:** The 9-for-9 exchange is genuinely balanced and creates real mutual dependency without any house being purely a giver or purely a receiver. The explicit "Cards from other houses you need" and "Cards you hold that other houses need" sections in the briefing are excellent design — every player knows exactly what they have to offer and what they need, which makes negotiation fast.

**Potential problem:** The Lens-Label self-destruct (Mission 2) creates a coordination requirement that cuts across the normal trading flow. All three houses should agree to run Mission 2 simultaneously (trade first, then all scan together), but nothing in the briefing prompts this coordination. A host prompt ("Before anyone scans a Lens-Label, get all three houses to agree on a scanning moment") would prevent a scenario where one house scans their labels and starts losing information while another house is still in a different trade conversation.

**Drake as Croft's heaviest counterpart:** Croft gives Drake 4 cards and gets 4 cards back from Drake. Jones gives Croft 5 and gets 5 from Croft. The trades are individually balanced but the Croft-Drake relationship is more complex — Drake and Croft are exchanging cards across more missions (Missions 1, 2, 3, 4, and the locked Mission 5 would also have required a Drake trade). This makes Drake Croft's primary trading partner. If Drake is slow or hostile, Croft is significantly blocked.

### Lockout consequence feedback

**Verdict: narratively proportional, mechanically clean, emotionally correct.**

The logical chain is airtight: Croft failed to secure the Grappling Rigs (Croft's technical identity) → the rigs are gone → the ledge is unreachable (climbing ledge, no holds) → Krane's compartment stays sealed. The consequence follows from who Croft is. It does not feel arbitrary.

The emotional weight is correct for one missed mission in five. Croft loses 20% of their missions, but the lost mission is not weighted equally in narrative terms — Yenus's hidden theory is the most tantalizing thing in the Croft packet, and not reading it is a genuine story loss, not just a mechanical one. The lockout has the right narrative sting without being catastrophic.

One note: in a real room, the lockout moment should happen publicly — the Croft player goes to the ledge (metaphorically: tries to scan or engage with Mission 5) and says "we can't reach it." The other players at the table witness this. That moment of social acknowledgment is where the consequence lands. If the lockout is read privately (player reads the brief, sees the LOCKED header, moves on), the narrative weight is halved.

The Yenus Riddle-Tablet I card — which Croft holds but cannot use — is the best design choice in the lockout. Holding the partial key makes the loss specific and visible, not abstract.
