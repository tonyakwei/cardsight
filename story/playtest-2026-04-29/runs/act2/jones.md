# Jones Junket — Act 2 Playtest

## Carry-forward state (Act 1 → Act 2)

Clean run — no lock consequences from Act 1. All five Act 2 missions available. Mission 1 (Ceremonial Whips) was completed in Act 1, so Mission 4 (Hanging Garden of Names) is unlocked. Entering Act 2 with the working theory that the lower temple was a school — a deliberately sealed institution of learning — and that the flooding was engineered by someone who wanted the school closed permanently. The central open question: who sealed it, and why was the sealing necessary? Act 1 left us with "institutional pedagogical" reading intact; Act 2's job is to break that frame.

### Mission 1: The Sealed Pantry

**Puzzle work:**

Jones holds Shelves 1 and 2. Needs Shelves 3+4 from Croft and Shelf 5 from Drake.

Known ingredients:
- Shelf 1: FRUIT + FIRE
- Shelf 2: OATS + STEW
- Shelf 3 (Croft): PLANT + GREASE
- Shelf 4 (Croft): HONEY + BREAD
- Shelf 5 (Drake): CREATURE + DRINK

The puzzle asks: "Name each preparation kept in this pantry." This means players must infer a specific culinary preparation from two ingredient labels. This is a word-knowledge puzzle — the answer to each shelf-pair is presumably a known English food term or culinary preparation that is canonically associated with those two ingredients.

Working through each:

**Shelf 1: FRUIT + FIRE**
FRUIT over FIRE → ROAST (fruit)? PRESERVE? JAM? COMPOTE? The canonical pantry product made by cooking fruit with fire is JAM or PRESERVE. COMPOTE is also strong. Without further constraints, JAM and PRESERVE are both equally valid. The designer needs to have picked one.
- Best guess: JAM (shortest, most common, pantry-shelf canonical word)
- Second guess: PRESERVE

**Shelf 2: OATS + STEW**
OATS in STEW → PORRIDGE is the most pantry-natural word, but porridge is typically OATS + WATER/MILK, not stew. GRUEL is oats in thin liquid (fits stew as liquid base). POTTAGE is the medieval term for grain-thickened stew. For a pre-modern temple context, POTTAGE is the most historically grounded answer.
- Best guess: PORRIDGE (recognizable)
- Second guess: POTTAGE (historically accurate)
- Problem: without knowing the designer's word, both are defensible

**Shelf 3: PLANT + GREASE**
PLANT + GREASE in a medicinal context → SALVE (plant-based grease applied to skin). POULTICE (plant matter + oil base). LINIMENT. In a temple-infirmary context, SALVE is the most canonical answer.
- Best guess: SALVE

**Shelf 4: HONEY + BREAD**
HONEY + BREAD → HONEYBREAD? TOAST is not a preparation. The most pantry-natural word for a honey-and-bread combination is a preserved loaf, a CAKE, or simply HONEY BREAD. In a single-word format: CAKE is possible (honey cake). MEAD is honey + water fermented, not bread. PUDDING uses bread as a component.
- Best guess: CAKE (honey cake is a real preparation)
- Second guess: LOAF

**Shelf 5: CREATURE + DRINK**
CREATURE + DRINK → BROTH (rendered from a creature, used as a drink). TONIC. DRAUGHT. In a medicinal pantry context: BROTH is the canonical animal-derived liquid preparation used as sustenance and medicine.
- Best guess: BROTH

**Final answer:** JAM PORRIDGE SALVE CAKE BROTH

**Confidence:** Low (each individual answer is a guess without designer constraints)
**Difficulty:** 4 (not a puzzle difficulty — a knowledge-gap difficulty; the mechanic is simple but the answers are arbitrary without a constraint system)
**Time-to-solve guess:** 15–25 minutes for a real team, dominated not by solving but by argument over which word is canonical
**Solving mode:** Guessed — the mechanic is understood but the answer space is unconstrained
**Trade need:** Shelves 3+4 from Croft, Shelf 5 from Drake. Jones must give Croft: Seven Calculation Tablet, Nine Calculation Tablet, Krane's Compartment Tile Slot 2, Purple Wall Tile 3, Lens-Label (Cluster 5). Jones must give Drake: Apothecary Note Ink-stained, Drevu's Compartment Tile Slot 2, Red Wall Tile 2, Bark Label (Stations 3+4), Togom Riddle-Tablet II. The Shelf 5 card is one card from Drake; Shelves 3+4 are two cards from Croft. Jones is giving significantly more than it is receiving for this mission alone — 5 cards out for 3 cards in.
**Sticking points:**
- The core design problem: FRUIT+FIRE, OATS+STEW etc. are ingredient pairs that each map to multiple plausible preparation names. JAM vs. PRESERVE vs. COMPOTE for Shelf 1 alone has three defensible answers. Without a constraint printed on the clue cards that restricts the answer vocabulary — e.g., a list of preparation names to match, or the first letter of the answer, or a category hint — players have no mechanism to converge on the designer's intended word.
- The mission format says "preparation name" but does not define what counts as a preparation name. "HONEY CAKE," "HONEYCAKE," "HONEY BREAD," and "CAKE" are all non-equivalent answers the system might accept or reject differently.
- "CREATURE + DRINK" is a notably abstract pair. CREATURE does not specify animal, bird, fish, or insect; DRINK does not specify water, alcohol, or broth. This pair could produce TONIC, BROTH, MEAD, ALE, CORDIAL, or SOUP. The spread is too wide.
- There is no self-checking mechanism. Unlike Mission 3 in Act 1 (where wrong ordering produced non-words), here a wrong answer is indistinguishable from a right one at the player level. The team will guess, enter, and not know whether they were wrong or just rejected by exact-match.

**Felt earned?:** No — there is no earning moment. The mechanic is "know a culinary word that fits these two ingredients." That is domain knowledge, not puzzle-solving. If the pairs were more constrained (e.g., FRUIT + FIRE + a letter count clue), the answer space would narrow enough to feel solvable.

**Designer flags:**
- CRITICAL: Define the answer constraint system. Either (a) provide the first letter of each preparation name on the shelf cards, (b) print a word bank somewhere in the mission text (select from: ...), or (c) rewrite the pairs so each pair uniquely maps to one widely-known preparation (e.g., GRAPE+FIRE → WINE, because fermentation from grapes is canonical and wine is the only common pantry word).
- CRITICAL: Specify the exact expected format. Are compound words allowed? Is "HONEY BREAD" one answer token or two? Does the answer field accept partial answers?
- The self-destruct mechanic is not on this card — but the pantry's ingredient cards probably should have some character to make scanning them feel urgent. As written, Mission 1 is the most mechanical and least narratively alive mission in the act.
- The lore question: what does it mean that the QRians kept a formal pantry of named preparations? This is the first suggestion that they were operating a functioning institution with food supply chains, not just a religious order performing rituals. The word "intention" in the story blurb is doing important narrative work — "eating with intention" suggests the preparations were pharmacological or ceremonial, not merely nutritional. The puzzle answer words should reflect this — JAM and PORRIDGE feel too mundane for a civilization that was "being influenced by something."

### Mission 2: The Wall of Repetitions, Amber

**Puzzle work:**

Jones holds Amber Wall Tile 1: `FHYSPKACQOVCEJADERSWIXE` (23 characters)

The mechanic: three tiles, each a 23-character degraded rewrite of the same inscription. At every position, two tiles preserve the original letter; one tile has drifted. Majority vote (2-out-of-3) at each position yields the original.

Jones cannot solve this alone. The tile in hand is: `F H Y S P K A C Q O V C E J A D E R S W I X E`

Without Tiles 2 (Drake) and 3 (Croft), I can analyze the mechanic but cannot reconstruct the inscription.

**Mechanic analysis:** The majority-vote mechanic is sound and well-designed. It scales naturally: with 3 tiles and at most 1 drift per position, the truth is always recoverable by simple comparison. The players' task is column-by-column letter comparison across three physical strings of 23 characters. This requires careful alignment — the strings must be laid in parallel and read position by position. The primary failure mode is misalignment (players comparing character 3 of one tile to character 4 of another). Tile length (23 chars) is long enough to be meaningful but short enough to fit on a QR card without truncation.

**Expected output:** A 23-character phrase in English. At 23 characters, this could be something like "THE SOURCE SPEAKS TO ALL" (22 chars) or "THEY COULD NOT STOP WRITING" (25 — too long) or "WE WROTE BECAUSE IT ASKED" (25 — too long). A 23-character phrase: "CORRUPTION FILLS THE WALL" = 24. "IT TOLD US WHAT TO CARVE" = 24. "THE SOURCE WROTE THROUGH US" = 26. "THEY COULD NOT STOP" = 19. "THEY WROTE WHAT IT SAID" = 22. "WE WROTE WHAT THEY SAID" = 22. "IT MADE US WRITE THESE" = 21. "THE WALL WROTE ITSELF" = 21.

Likely 23-char phrase: "WE WROTE BECAUSE IT COMMANDED" is too long. "IT COMMANDED WE INSCRIBE" = 24. "WRITTEN BY WHAT DWELLS HERE" = 26. "THE VOICE BENEATH THE STONE" = 26.

Given the drift-mechanic is symmetric across all three walls (Amber/Red/Purple) and each wall produces a line, the three lines together form the full inscription. The amber wall is likely line 2 (the middle line, thematically). Given Tile 1 starts with `FHYSP...` the F is probably correct (appears on at least one tile; majority will confirm or deny). The inscription likely opens with a word like "FOR" or "FROM" or "FILLED."

**Speculative reconstruction from Tile 1 only:** `F H Y S P K A C Q O V C E J A D E R S W I X E` — this is almost certainly not the clean inscription; many of these characters are probably drift positions. Characters like Q, X, J are rare in English and likely drift positions. Probable original letters at those positions: Q→O or U, X→E or S, J→I. Working from that: `F H ? S P K A C ? O V C E ? A D E R S W I ? E` — still unresolvable without majority data.

**Final answer:** Cannot commit — requires Tile 2 from Drake and Tile 3 from Croft.
**Committed guess (for playtest purposes):** WITHELD — flagged as trade-dependent, cannot resolve solo

**Confidence:** N/A (tile-dependent)
**Difficulty:** 2 (once tiles are pooled — the mechanic itself is simple column comparison)
**Time-to-solve guess:** 8–12 minutes once all three tiles are in hand; the physical act of laying 23×3 = 69 characters in a grid and voting is straightforward
**Solving mode:** Understood-mechanic (mechanic is clear; missing data only)
**Trade need:** Amber Wall Tile 2 from Drake, Amber Wall Tile 3 from Croft
**Sticking points:**
- **No sticking points in the mechanic** — majority-vote is elegant and self-explaining. This is well-designed.
- **Alignment risk:** Players writing out three 23-character strings must align them perfectly. A single misalignment propagates error to every subsequent position. Suggest the cards have position numbers printed above each character (1–23), or at minimum have characters grouped in blocks of 5 with a space (like a bank account number) for easier counting.
- **The inscription will be 23 characters.** English phrases at exactly 23 characters are unusual. If the phrase uses word spaces, those spaces must be recoverable through the majority mechanic (the drift letters at space positions must not accidentally spell something). Consider whether the inscription is written with or without spaces — without spaces is safer for the mechanic (no alignment ambiguity at word boundaries) but harder to read.
- **Cross-house reveal:** The mission says Drake reads Red, Croft reads Purple, and when the host calls it, all three are combined. This is a strong narrative moment but requires the host to orchestrate the reveal. If one house finishes significantly faster, they'll be sitting on a line of the inscription with nothing to do while another house works. The host should have guidance on pacing (stall, give a hint, etc.).

**Felt earned?:** Yes (conditionally) — if the mechanic executes cleanly, the three-line inscription reveal is the strongest narrative payoff in the act. The majority-vote mechanic is fair, learnable, and satisfying to apply.

**Designer flags:**
- Add position markers or grouping to the tile strings to reduce misalignment errors.
- The cross-house reveal depends on host orchestration; document this in host notes, not just mission notes. The host needs to know when to call "combine your answers now."
- The 23-character length is unusual. Verify this was chosen to produce a specific phrase and that the phrase at that length is grammatically clean without forced abbreviation.

**[STORY LOG ENTRY 2: Theory after Mission 2]**

The Wall of Repetitions is the most significant evidence so far. The QRians wrote the same phrase hundreds of times — floor to ceiling. This is not instruction, not curriculum, not archival. Repetition at that scale is compulsive. The word "confession" in the story blurb is explicit. Something made them write. The "drift" — individual chisels eroding differently — suggests the inscription was carved over time by multiple carvers, each degrading it slightly, each trying to reproduce a source text they may have been reading from memory rather than from the original. They were copying, not composing. And they could not stop. This is the first moment the frame shifts. This is not "meticulous scholars who documented everything." This is people who were compelled. The Source — whatever it is — starts to feel like an external pressure, not an internal discipline. First crack in the institutional model.

### Mission 3: Vesh's Compartment

**Puzzle work:**

Jones holds Slot 2:
- Procedure I, step 2: **branch**
- Procedure II, step 2: **tingle**

Drake holds Slot 1:
- Procedure I, step 1: **trunk**
- Procedure II, step 1: **pollen**

Croft holds Slot 3:
- Procedure I, step 3: **leaf**
- Procedure II, step 3: **gasp**

**Assembled sequences:**

Procedure I: trunk → branch → leaf → **?**

This is an unambiguous botanical sequence moving from structural/large to structural/small in plant anatomy. Trunk is the main body. Branch subdivides from trunk. Leaf attaches to branch. The next step continuing inward or smaller: SEED (the reproductive unit contained within leaf tissue, on the tree)? BUD (a leaf-precursor, but it comes before leaf)? PETAL (flower component, but not in this progression style)?

More carefully: trunk → branch → leaf follows the vascular system of a plant from the center outward, and also follows a size-decreasing progression. Continuing outward/smaller from leaf: the natural next step is BUD (a leaf-bud, the smallest structural unit of the same system) or SEED. But BUD logically precedes LEAF (buds open into leaves), not follows it.

Alternative frame: this is a word-association or semantic chain, not necessarily botanical anatomy. Trunk → branch → leaf → BOOK? (tree parts leading to paper/book — a clever jump). Or: trunk, branch, leaf are all parts of a tree; the fourth term is the one thing a tree produces that isn't structural: FRUIT? FLOWER? SEED?

The most likely designed answer is **SEED** (smallest unit, reproductive, logically terminal in the botanical sequence from large to small structural elements).

Second candidate: **ROOT** — if the sequence is not directional (outward from core) but rather completes a set of "the four principal named parts of a tree." Trunk + Branch + Leaf + Root = the four parts. ROOT feels like the most satisfying "complete the set" answer for a puzzle.

**Committed answer for Procedure I: SEED** (primary) or ROOT (secondary)

---

Procedure II: pollen → tingle → gasp → **?**

This sequence is sensory/physiological, describing escalating physical responses. This is a classic allergic reaction progression: pollen enters the body → causes tingle (nasal or throat sensation) → causes gasp (shortness of breath, allergic response) → the next step is...

SNEEZE — the canonical physical output following tingle/gasp in an allergic/respiratory sequence. Pollen → tingle (nasal irritation) → gasp (intake) → SNEEZE (expulsion). This is the most mechanically satisfying completion.

Alternative: WHEEZE (an asthma-type continuation of gasp). COUGH. RASH (if we're moving to a different symptom type).

But SNEEZE is the single most naturally completing word for a sequence that ends in GASP from POLLEN exposure. It also has the virtue of being unexpected-but-earned — the puzzle is describing the physiological response to pollen inhalation in real time.

**Committed answer for Procedure II: SNEEZE**

**Final answer:** SEED SNEEZE

**Confidence:** Medium for SEED (ROOT is the main alternative), High for SNEEZE
**Difficulty:** 3
**Time-to-solve guess:** 10–15 minutes (getting all tiles, then spending time arguing about Procedure I's terminal word)
**Solving mode:** Understood-mechanic for Procedure II; inference/debate for Procedure I
**Trade need:** Slot 1 (trunk/pollen) from Drake, Slot 3 (leaf/gasp) from Croft. Without the outer slots, neither sequence is solvable — all four position words are required to see the pattern. This is the best-calibrated trade dependency in the act: you genuinely cannot solve without all three tiles.
**Sticking points:**

1. **Procedure I has two strong competing answers.** SEED and ROOT are both defensible terminal words for a trunk→branch→leaf sequence. ROOT is compelling as "complete the set of named tree parts." SEED is compelling as "next step in the outward botanical progression." The designer must have intended one — but without knowing the design intent, a team will split and may submit the wrong one. If the answer is ROOT, the sequence is not directional (smallest-to-largest or largest-to-smallest) which means teams reasoning about direction will guess wrong. If the answer is SEED, teams reasoning about "complete the set" will guess ROOT.

2. **Procedure II is strong.** SNEEZE completes the allergic response chain cleanly and the chain is memorable. This is the better-designed of the two procedures.

3. **"Vesh" is introduced as a name here.** Who is Vesh? The mission calls it "Vesh's Compartment" and the story blurb says "Vesh logged something this place wanted forgotten." This is the first named QRian individual. The compartment is a personal record — Vesh's private observations or logs. What was "wanted forgotten"? The puzzle answer words (SEED SNEEZE) are not obviously "forgotten" material — they're botanical and physiological. The narrative hook ("wanted forgotten") is not paid off by the answer. This is a story coherence gap.

4. **The blank passcode slot framing is strong.** "Step 4 of each procedure is blank. Your job is to infer the word that completes the progression." This is a clean mission brief — the mechanic is immediately understood. No friction in comprehension.

**Felt earned?:** Partial — Procedure II is fully earned. Procedure I has answer ambiguity that undercuts the earning moment.

**Designer flags:**
- Add one more constraint to Procedure I to disambiguate SEED vs. ROOT. The simplest fix: include the first letter of the answer on the blank slot tile (it's supposed to be blank, but a chiseled letter-count or a partial glyph would steer without giving away). Alternatively, reframe the sequence to make the direction unambiguous (e.g., SEED → SPROUT → SAPLING → TREE would go the other direction and terminate clearly in something larger, making TREE unambiguous).
- Vesh's narrative hook ("wanted forgotten") is not paid off by the puzzle mechanic. The compartment should contain something that feels prohibited or suppressed. Botanical sequences and allergic responses do not feel like forbidden knowledge. Consider whether the compartment should reveal something about the Source directly — e.g., Vesh documented that the repetitive writing was not voluntary. That would make the "wanted forgotten" framing land.

**[STORY LOG ENTRY 3: Theory after Mission 3]**

Vesh kept private records. The table's procedure-rows document something Vesh observed — a botanical sequence and a sensory response sequence. Why would a botanist's observation be "something the temple wanted forgotten"? Unless the botanical sequence is not about plants — unless "trunk, branch, leaf, seed" is a metaphor for how the Source propagates through people: from a central body (trunk) through networks (branch) to individuals (leaf) to new carriers (seed). And pollen → tingle → gasp → sneeze could be describing how exposure to the Source begins: airborne pollen, initial contact, respiratory response. Vesh documented that the Source spreads like a plant — or like an allergen. That's not a botanical lesson. That's an infection model. This is the first moment I consider that the QRians' constant writing might not have been voluntary. Vesh knew it. That's why it was sealed.

### Mission 4: The Hanging Garden of Names

**Puzzle work:**

This mission is available (Ceremonial Whips retrieved in Act 1). Jones holds Burial-Rite Fragment, Folded (closers). Needs Charred (openers) from Croft and Half-Erased (carriers) from Drake.

The mechanic: three cards each describe a class of glyphs (closers, openers, carriers), each with a strike sequence. Each sequence traces a letter on the canopy map. Three letters → one English word.

Jones's card content: "Strike first the **speakers** — every glyph that closes the mouth. The dead spoke our names back to us in the order we forgot them; we wrote each name on the bone that had carried it. Crack each in turn."

This describes closers as "glyphs that close the mouth" — phonetically, these are consonants or specific articulation-close sounds (B, P, M in phonetics close the lips; stops close airflow). The fragment says to mark every closer-class glyph on the canopy map with sequence numbers and crack them in numerical order — the path traces a letter.

Without the canopy map, the mechanic is clear but the actual letter cannot be determined. The same applies to the other two cards. The three letters must form a recognizable English word.

**What can be inferred:**
- Three-letter English word from three glyph classes: openers, carriers, closers
- Each glyph class traces one letter
- The word likely has narrative significance — it will name something that "hangs in this garden" (the mission question)

Plausible three-letter answers given the lore context: GOD, ASH, DIM, FAR, AGE, END, OLD, NEW, ONE, ALL, WAY, THE, KEY, LAW, SIN, LIE. Given the garden is a Hall of Names (burial-rite fragments, tagged vines with names), the word probably describes a concept related to naming, memory, or death: SOUL? (4 letters) — no. GOD (3 letters) fits the Source mythology. ASH (the vines, the crumbling). ONE (the Source as singular). WAR, SIN, LIE — if the garden names people who died for something.

Cannot commit to an answer without the canopy map.

**Final answer:** Cannot commit — map and two additional cards required

**Confidence:** N/A
**Difficulty:** 4 (the mechanic is sophisticated — glyph-class-sorted strike sequences tracing letterforms on a physical map is the most visually demanding puzzle in the act)
**Time-to-solve guess:** 20–35 minutes for a live team (the canopy map physical artifact adds a layer of hands-on interpretation that will dominate)
**Solving mode:** Cannot solve — missing data
**Trade need:** Burial-Rite Fragment, Charred (openers) from Croft; Burial-Rite Fragment, Half-Erased (carriers) from Drake. AND requires the printed canopy map physical artifact.
**Sticking points:**

1. **The canopy map is a critical artifact not present in the mission brief.** The mission says "You have a printed overhead map of the canopy" — this is a physical artifact that must be printed and distributed. If it's not in the player's hands, the puzzle is completely unsolvable. The brief does not describe the map's contents, which means this playtest cannot simulate solving the mission without it. This is the most physically-dependent puzzle in the act.

2. **60-second self-destruct on the Burial-Rite Fragment cards.** Closers, openers, and carriers must all be read and written down before the cards destroy themselves. In a live room, this means all three cards must be scanned nearly simultaneously and their contents recorded quickly. The self-destruct creates real urgency (unlike the time pressure from sealing doors, which is abstract and ambient). However: if one player scans, reads, and writes, but another player hasn't scanned yet, the content is lost. Coordination is required before scanning begins. The host must brief players that these cards are time-limited BEFORE they scan — not after.

3. **The linguistic framing of "closers / openers / carriers"** is elegant. Phonetic articulation classes are real (stops, fricatives, sonorants). Players with linguistics background will map these immediately; players without will spend time parsing what "closes the mouth" means. The fragment's phrasing ("glyphs that close the mouth") is evocative but not technically precise enough to unambiguously identify which glyphs are "closers" without seeing the glyph system's full character map.

4. **Three letters from three glyph classes is a strong structural mechanic.** Each card contributes exactly one letter. No house can complete the word alone. This is the best example of genuine inter-house dependency in the act — all three houses MUST contribute to solve Mission 4. This is exactly the trading mechanic working as intended.

**Felt earned?:** Cannot assess without the canopy map. The structure suggests it should feel earned — the three-class glyph sort + letterform tracing is the most physically satisfying mechanic in the act. Whether the answer word pays off depends entirely on what it is.

**Designer flags:**
- The canopy map must be pre-printed and in the player packet before the act begins. It is not scannable — it is a physical artifact for hands-on solving. This should be called out explicitly in the mission briefing and in the host's setup notes. If any house forgets to bring it out, Mission 4 is blocked.
- The 60-second self-destruct coordination problem needs a host cue. Recommend the host announce: "Before you scan these, read your card descriptions together so you know what each person needs to write down. Then all three houses scan simultaneously."
- Clarify in the fragment text what "carriers" are phonetically — "openers" opens the mouth (vowels or aspirants), "closers" closes the mouth (stops/labials), but "carriers" is less obvious. Sonorants? Nasals? The glyph-class taxonomy should map cleanly to something players can identify.
- This is the strongest mechanic design in the act. Protect it from logistics failure (missing map, miscoordinated self-destruct).

**[STORY LOG ENTRY 4: Theory after Mission 4]**

The garden holds thousands of names — people buried, memorialized, their names written on bone-weighted clay. "The dead spoke our names back to us in the order we forgot them" — the burial rite says the dead teach the living which names they've lost. This is a civilization preoccupied with forgetting. Not just documenting things they knew, but repeatedly mourning things they were losing. The Wall of Repetitions was compulsive copying from memory. The garden is a monument to things being forgotten. The QRians were not just organized scholars — they were terrified of losing knowledge. Something was taking it from them. The Source, whatever it is, may not be filling them with knowledge but draining them of it. They wrote because they were forgetting. They carved the same phrase hundreds of times because they could not retain it. The "corruption" in the act title starts to make sense: not corruption of data, but corruption of memory, of cognitive function. The temple is a monument to civilizational amnesia.

### Mission 5: The Ceiling Inscription (Sefa's Riddles)

**Puzzle work:**

Jones holds Riddle-Tablet I: "It is said to flow, but it doesn't. Instead, it stands beautifully."

Need Riddle-Tablet II from Croft.

**Solving Tablet I:**

"It is said to flow, but it doesn't. Instead, it stands beautifully."

Things "said to flow":
- Time: "time flows" — but time doesn't literally flow, and "stands beautifully" doesn't obviously apply to time
- Water/river: rivers are said to flow — but rivers do flow, so the paradox of "said to flow but doesn't" requires a specific case
- Hair: described as "flowing" but actually stands (or hangs stationary when still) — "stands beautifully" works for hair
- Traffic: said to flow, sometimes stands (traffic standstill) — not poetic
- Electricity: said to flow as current — not relevant
- Language/conversation: said to flow — "stands beautifully" doesn't fit
- STILL water: water is said to flow, but still water doesn't flow — it stands. "Still" means both "not moving" and describes something beautiful (still life, still water). STILL as an answer would be a word answer, but the question asks for what the thing IS, not its state.

Most likely answer: **STILL** (as in "still water" — water is said to flow, but still water stands beautifully; and "still" as an adjective describes the quality of not flowing while standing)

Alternative: **STATUE** — a statue is "said to flow" in the sense that artists describe the flowing lines or movement captured in marble. A statue doesn't actually move — it stands. "Stands beautifully" is literally what a statue does. This is stronger than STILL because:
- "Said to flow" applies to artistic description of statuary (flowing robes, flowing hair, flowing poses)
- "Stands" is literal — a statue literally stands
- "Beautifully" is natural (beautiful statuary)
- The paradox resolves clearly: what appears to flow, actually stands

Revised primary answer: **STATUE**

But STATUE is 6 letters and feels long for a riddle answer in this context. A QRian riddle designed to seal a scroll-case might have a shorter canonical answer. STILL (5 letters) or STONE (5 letters).

STONE: "said to flow" — lava? magma is said to flow and then becomes stone. "Stands beautifully" — stone stands. This is decent but the "said to flow" framing doesn't conventionally describe stone.

**Committed answer for Tablet I: STILL** (water said to flow; still water stands; "stands beautifully" = still water as beautiful image)

Cannot solve Tablet II without Croft's card.

**Speculative Tablet II:** Croft holds it. Without content, cannot assess. For a paired-riddle puzzle where both answers are submitted together, both must be committed. Committing to STILL for Tablet I; Tablet II is trade-dependent.

**Final answer:** STILL [UNKNOWN] — committing STILL for Tablet I; Tablet II unresolved

**Confidence:** Medium for STILL (STATUE and STONE are real alternatives), N/A for Tablet II
**Difficulty:** 3 for Tablet I; unknown for Tablet II
**Time-to-solve guess:** 5–10 minutes per riddle once both tablets are in hand; the format (two riddles, two answers, separated by a space) is clean and self-explaining
**Solving mode:** Understood-mechanic for Tablet I; guessed among three candidates
**Trade need:** Sefa Riddle-Tablet II from Croft
**Sticking points:**

1. **Tablet I has three defensible answers (STILL, STATUE, STONE).** The riddle is well-formed — it has a genuine paradox ("said to flow, but doesn't") and a satisfying resolution quality ("stands beautifully"). But without a single unambiguous referent, teams will argue. STILL is the most common — "flows" vs. "stands still" is a natural opposition. STATUE is the most evocative. STONE is the most thematically resonant in a temple context.

2. **"Stands beautifully" is unusual phrasing.** In English, we say "stands still" (adverb), "stands tall," "stands proud," but "stands beautifully" is slightly awkward. This phrasing is carrying the riddle's entire resolution — if it's slightly off, the answer is harder to pin down. "Stands in stillness" or "stands perfectly still" would land more cleanly while preserving the paradox.

3. **Sefa as a named QRian.** "That's a name. Sefa wanted to be found." — the ceiling inscription is personal. This is the second named individual (after Vesh). Sefa left a riddle as a calling card. Why riddles? If the Source compelled the QRians to write, Sefa may have encoded information in riddle form specifically to preserve it from being overwritten by compulsive repetition. Riddles require understanding, not copying. This makes Sefa a figure of resistance — someone who found a way to communicate that bypassed the compulsion. This is excellent narrative work and the mission brief earns it. The story hook is the strongest in the act.

4. **The paired-answer format (two words separated by a space) is the same format as Mission 3 (Vesh's Compartment).** Two missions in a row with "two words separated by a space" format. This may be intentional (both are compartment-unsealing puzzles) but creates monotony in answer format. If the answers are SEED SNEEZE and STILL [X], both are two-word entries to unlock containers. Consider whether the format differentiation is worth pursuing.

**Felt earned?:** Partial for Tablet I — STILL is satisfying if correct, but the multi-answer ambiguity prevents full confidence. The narrative frame around Sefa is the best story beat in Act 2 and fully earns its position as the final mission.

**Designer flags:**
- Add a constraint to the Tablet I riddle that eliminates two of the three candidate answers. The cleanest fix: end with "...and its name begins with S, ends with L" — immediately narrows to STILL (STATUE ends in E, STONE ends in E). Or change the final clause to something only STILL satisfies: "It is said to flow, but it stands — and its stillness is the point." (The word "stillness" in the clue makes STILL the unambiguous answer.)
- Sefa's narrative hook is the best writing in Act 2. This mission should be the emotional climax. If Tablet II is comparably strong, the paired reveal will be memorable. Protect this from logistics friction — getting Tablet II from Croft should be a high-value, motivated trade.
- The scroll-case behind the loose tile: what does it contain? If it's a content card with narrative payoff, that payoff should be proportional to the setup ("Sefa wanted to be found"). If it just says "mission complete," the reveal underperforms its setup.

Sefa left her riddles as a trap door in the wall of compulsion — something that required genuine thought, not mechanical repetition. She expected someone to find them eventually. This is deliberate communication, encoded to survive whatever was compelling the others. Sefa knew the writing was compelled. She found a workaround. The scroll-case behind the tile is presumably her real message — not encoded in glyphs, but in something the Source couldn't easily reach. By the end of Act 2, the frame has fully shifted. ### Plausibility check:

Jones gives 10 cards and receives 9. The raw count is roughly balanced. However, the balance of urgency is not symmetric:

**Imbalance 1 — Drake:** Drake needs 5 cards from Jones but Jones only needs 4 from Drake. Drake has a stronger dependence on Jones than Jones has on Drake. In a real room, Jones has more negotiating leverage over Drake than Drake has over Jones. Drake will need to offer favorable terms for the Togom Riddle-Tablet II and the Bark Label cards. This could be interesting (Jones extorts a better deal) or friction-generating (Drake feels exploited).

However, the cards Jones needs from Croft cluster into two late missions (Mission 4 and 5), which means Croft has strategic timing leverage — they could delay sending Burial-Rite Charred until they've confirmed Jones sent them the Calculation Tablets. This means the trade for Mission 4 must complete before scanning begins, and all three houses must be ready to scan at the same moment. If Jones is still waiting for Drake's Half-Erased fragment when Croft scans their Charred fragment, Croft's card self-destructs before Jones can record it. The self-destruct mechanics makes Mission 4's trading window extremely tight. The host must coordinate this explicitly.

**Overall plausibility:** The trading negotiation is feasible in theory. The card counts are roughly balanced (10 out, 9 in). **Verdict: The trading mechanic is well-calibrated for Jones specifically, but Mission 4's self-destruct timing makes it the riskiest trade to execute in a live room.**

## End-of-act theory of the temple

The QRians were a civilization of scholars who built their upper temple as a working institution — pantry, archive, garden, writing chamber. They were methodical, literate, institutionalized. But something in this level infected them. The most likely vector is environmental: airborne pollen, mineral presence in the stone, or something concentrated in the Green Department's atmosphere. Vesh's Compartment documents the physiological pathway — pollen contact → tingle → gasp → sneeze — like a medical observation of an exposure event. Once exposed, the QRians could not stop writing. The Wall of Repetitions is the record of this: the same phrase, hundreds of times, different carvers degrading it slightly with each pass, because they were copying from memory rather than from text. They were trying to hold onto something and losing it with each transcription.

Sefa understood this and worked around it — her riddles require genuine comprehension, not mechanical copying, and she encoded them to survive. The garden of names is a monument to amnesia: the dead speak names back to the living because the living are forgetting names faster than they can learn them.

The temple was not sealed from within. It was sealed from outside, or by the last lucid QRians, to prevent whatever was causing the compulsion from spreading outward. "Those who stayed too long were sealed away forever" — from Act 1 — is the self-aware record of this: the inscription was written by people who knew they had stayed too long, who knew the consequence, and who wrote it down as their cognitive function degraded. The sealing of the temple is an act of quarantine, not abandonment. The Source — whatever it is — is not supernatural but environmental: something in the stone, the air, or the water of the Green Department that rewrites cognition over time.

The institutional-to-environmental shift fully landed by Mission 2 (Wall of Repetitions: "confession," compulsive copying) and was confirmed by Mission 3 (Vesh's infection model) and Mission 4 (amnesia monument). The shift happened faster than expected — Act 2 is efficient in this respect.

## Designer Summary

### Hardest puzzles (ranked)

1. **Mission 4 (Hanging Garden of Names):** The canopy map physical artifact is a single point of failure. Without it, the puzzle is insoluble. The self-destruct coordination requirement adds live-room logistics complexity no other mission approaches. Even with the map and cards, tracing letterforms from glyph-class strike sequences is the most cognitively demanding task in the act.
2. **Mission 1 (Sealed Pantry):** Not difficult in the puzzle sense — but hardest to get a correct answer because the answer space is unconstrained. Teams can reason correctly and still miss because JAM vs. PRESERVE vs. COMPOTE is unanswerable without the designer's key. Difficulty-by-ambiguity is the worst kind.
3. **Mission 3 (Vesh's Compartment):** Procedure I's terminal word (SEED vs. ROOT) is ambiguous. Procedure II (SNEEZE) is well-defined and clean.
4. **Mission 5 (Sefa's Riddles):** Tablet I has three candidate answers (STILL, STATUE, STONE). Well-formed riddle, multiple valid solutions.
5. **Mission 2 (Wall of Repetitions):** Easiest puzzle once tiles are pooled. The majority-vote mechanic is self-explanatory and self-checking. The main risk is misalignment in the 23-character string.

### Puzzles with mechanic/clue/answer problems (specific)

**Mission 1 — Sealed Pantry [CRITICAL]:**
- Five ingredient pairs map to unconstrained culinary answer words. FRUIT+FIRE → JAM/PRESERVE/COMPOTE; OATS+STEW → PORRIDGE/GRUEL/POTTAGE; PLANT+GREASE → SALVE/POULTICE/LINIMENT; HONEY+BREAD → CAKE/LOAF/PUDDING; CREATURE+DRINK → BROTH/TONIC/DRAUGHT.
- No self-checking mechanism. Wrong answers are indistinguishable from right ones at the player level.
- Required fix: add a word bank, first-letter constraints, or rewrite pairs to uniquely specify one well-known preparation each.

**Mission 3 — Vesh's Compartment [moderate]:**
- Procedure I: trunk→branch→leaf→? accepts SEED and ROOT equally. Both are defensible, only one is correct. Need one additional constraint (letter count, letter hint, or sequence rewrite) to disambiguate.
- The "wanted forgotten" narrative frame is not paid off by botanical/physiological answers. If the compartment is meant to feel dangerous or suppressed, the answers should feel more sensitive than "SEED SNEEZE."

**Mission 5 — Sefa's Riddles [moderate]:**
- Tablet I: "said to flow, but stands beautifully" → STILL / STATUE / STONE all work. "Stands beautifully" is awkward phrasing and adds ambiguity rather than resolving it. Rewrite the final clause to force a single answer.

**Mission 4 — Hanging Garden [logistics]:**
- Canopy map must be a physical artifact in the player packet; it is not described in the clue cards and cannot be improvised during play. If absent, mission fails completely.
- 60-second self-destruct on all three Burial-Rite Fragments requires pre-coordinated simultaneous scanning from all three houses. This must be in host notes and in the mission briefing.
- "Carriers" phonetic class is not as self-defining as "closers" and "openers" — add clarification in the fragment text.

### Puzzles that worked well

**Mission 2 (Wall of Repetitions):** The majority-vote mechanic is the best-designed mechanical puzzle in the act. Elegant, scalable, self-checking (errors produce non-words or obvious nonsense), and structurally requires cross-house trading. The three-wall three-line inscription reveal is the strongest narrative payoff moment in the act. Alignment grouping on the tile strings (group in blocks of 5) would be the only improvement needed.

**Mission 3, Procedure II (pollen→tingle→gasp→?):** SNEEZE is the cleanest, most satisfying terminal word in the act. The allergic response chain is memorable and the answer is earned. This demonstrates the right design for "sequence completion" puzzles — a unique, defensible, satisfying answer.

**Mission 5 narrative frame (Sefa):** The "Sefa wanted to be found" framing is the strongest story beat in the act. Riddles as a resistance strategy against compelled-copying is clever and thematically resonant. If the scroll-case payoff matches the setup, this is the best mission in Act 2.

### Story coherence

The Source mythology landed. The institutional→environmental shift completed during Mission 2 (Wall of Repetitions) and was structurally confirmed by Mission 3 (Vesh's infection model). By Mission 4 (garden as amnesia monument), the full frame was visible: the QRians wrote because they were forgetting, not because they were meticulous. The time pressure from sealing doors reinforces that this is an environment that reacts to human presence — the temple seals itself the longer you stay, echoing "those who stayed too long were sealed away forever."

**When did "something is wrong with these people" shift happen?** Mid-Mission 2. The word "confession" in the story blurb, combined with "hundreds of stacked rewrites, each eroded in its own way," is the inflection point. The compulsive-copying explanation is forced at that moment. Everything that follows reinforces it.

**Where are the gaps?**
- The Source itself is still undefined. What is it physically? Where is it in the temple? What does it want (if anything)? Act 2 gives the phenomenology (compelled writing, memory loss, respiratory exposure) but not the object. This may be intentional — Act 3 presumably makes the Source visible — but by end of Act 2, players asking "but what IS it?" will be frustrated if they don't get something more concrete.
- Vesh's compartment: why "wanted forgotten"? The botanical and physiological observations Vesh recorded are scientifically logical (an infection model), but they don't feel forbidden. If Vesh documented "the Source is not supernatural — it is a spore" or something specific, that would feel worth suppressing. SEED SNEEZE as answers implies the spore/pollen model is correct — but the mission brief doesn't tell players this explicitly.
- The pantry (Mission 1) doesn't connect to the Source mythology at all. PLANT+GREASE, CREATURE+DRINK — these could be pharmacological preparations designed to counteract the Source's effects, or they could just be food. The lore opportunity is wasted. If the mission brief said "the QRians kept substances here that were meant to protect against exposure" it would make Mission 1 narratively load-bearing.

### Pacing

**Time pressure:** The sealing-doors time pressure is present atmospherically but never mechanically enforced. The only mission with real time pressure is Mission 4's 60-second self-destruct. Everything else could be solved at leisure if players ignore the ambient narrative. The time pressure feels fake for Missions 1, 2, 3, and 5. Real for Mission 4.

**Where a team stalls:** Mission 1 (unconstrained answer words — they'll argue). Mission 4 (if canopy map is missing or self-destruct is mis-coordinated). Mission 3 (SEED vs. ROOT disagreement).

**Where a team rushes too fast:** Mission 2 (majority-vote is quick once tiles are pooled — they may misalign the strings in their hurry). Mission 5 (riddles are fast to attempt but may lock out early on wrong guesses if the answer system doesn't give useful feedback).

**Overall act pacing:** The missions increase in physical complexity (Mission 2 is column comparison → Mission 4 is canopy map tracing). Narrative complexity also escalates (Mission 1 is institutional → Mission 5 is individual resistance). The pacing architecture is well-designed. The main risk is Mission 1 stalling the whole act at the start if the answer-word problem is not fixed.

### Trading mechanic feedback

Act 2 is a significant improvement over Act 1's zero-trade situation. Jones cannot complete any mission without trading. The dependencies are:

- Jones → Croft: 5 cards out (load-bearing for Croft's missions)
- Jones → Drake: 5 cards out (load-bearing for Drake's missions)
- Croft → Jones: 5 cards in (required for Jones's Missions 1, 2, 3, 4, 5)
- Drake → Jones: 4 cards in (required for Jones's Missions 1, 2, 3, 4)

**Calibration assessment:** Roughly balanced numerically. The strategic imbalance is that Jones holds 10 cards other houses need, making Jones a hub. In a real room, this means other houses approach Jones. Jones is positioned as the "decoder house" (academic, meticulous, holds analysis cards), which fits its identity — Jones holds tiles, tablets, wall segments, calculation sheets. This is coherent with the house identity.

**Risk:** Jones is giving Togom Riddle-Tablet II to Drake for Drake's missions. If Drake's missions are blocked by other trade failures, Drake may not prioritize completing the trade with Jones. Jones should be aware of this dependency chain.

**The one-sidedness:** Some trades are genuinely symmetric (Jones gives Drake Red Wall Tile 2; Jones receives Amber Wall Tile 2 from Drake — both need the other's tile for the same mission type, Wall of Repetitions). Others are less naturally linked (Jones gives Drake Bark Label for Reagent Alcove; Jones receives Drake's Burial-Rite Half-Erased for Hanging Garden — these are for different missions with no reciprocal logic). In a real room, unlinked trades require more explicit negotiation ("I'll give you this if you give me that") rather than the natural "we both need each other's tile" logic. Both types are present, which is healthy.

**Verdict: Trading mechanic is well-calibrated for Act 2. Mission 4's self-destruct coordination problem is the highest-risk logistics point. Fix Mission 1's answer ambiguity before live play or it will poison the trading energy at the start of the act.**
