# Date-Night Story Games

Working draft for short two-player CardSight experiences built for an apartment date setting.

This document captures the current design learnings, the intended play loop, and the latest rewrite of `The Spyhouse`.

## Date Goal

Build a short apartment-based date experience that feels:
- playful
- mysterious
- immersive
- revealing without becoming heavy-handed

The cards should help two people tell a shared story while quietly learning how the other person thinks, notices, improvises, values, and reacts.

The intended result is not "winning" a game. The intended result is:
- creating chemistry
- giving the night a distinct shape
- making disclosure feel natural
- leaving behind a memorable little world the two people made together

## Philosophy

### Fiction as cover

The fictional frame exists to make honesty easier.

Players are not asked to answer direct personal questions in a vacuum. Instead, they smuggle real things through the scene:
- what they notice
- what they distrust
- what they admire
- what they are good at
- what makes them stay

### The real person is the signal

The game works best when prompts target the real person at the table, not only the fictional role they are playing.

Instead of asking:
- what would your character notice?

Prefer:
- what would you notice first?
- what would you find suspicious?
- what would make you trust someone?
- how do you try to seem calm?

### Pressure, not questionnaires

Each card should add pressure to the current scene:
- a clue
- a mistake
- a tonal shift
- a confession leak
- a new interpretation

The card should not feel like a form to fill out.

### Immersion over explanation

The phone should feel like a private signal source, not a facilitator explaining the rules every turn.

The strongest rhythm is:
1. Scan.
2. Read silently.
3. Introduce the pressure in-world.
4. Respond in-world.
5. Stay with the moment until it feels spent.

### Interpretation is part of the fun

The fun is not only what someone says. It is what their choices imply about them.

That means the games are partly about reading:
- why that detail stood out
- why that object became important
- why that lie took that shape
- why that ending felt right

## Design Goal

Use fiction as cover for real disclosure.

The story should feel playful, mysterious, and immersive, but the interesting material should come from what the real people at the table choose to notice, protect, admire, distrust, or admit.

The game should not feel like:
- a questionnaire
- an improv workshop
- a sequence of instructions for both players

The game should feel like:
- a scene that keeps getting pressured by new information
- a fictional world that absorbs real instincts
- a conversation where character and self blur on purpose

## Current Learnings

### 1. Fiction needs a container

An open-ended "tell a story together" prompt is too blank for a date-night game.

The experience gets better when the story has:
- a location
- an objective
- a missing thing
- a reason both people are present
- a reason personal details can become evidence

This is why `The Spyhouse` works better than the looser false-name version. The safehouse apartment, missing package, and staged meeting give the players something to push against.

### 2. Cards should pressure the scanner, not choreograph both players

The second player should not get a separate assignment on most cards.

Instead:
- the scanner reads the card silently
- the scanner introduces the new clue, shift, or pressure in-world
- the other player responds in-world by accepting, questioning, complicating, or reinterpreting it

This keeps the game from feeling procedural.

### 3. Prompts should target the real person, not only the fictional character

Weak version:
- "Make it the kind of detail your character would notice."

Stronger version:
- "Choose the kind of detail you would notice first in a room like this."

The fiction matters, but it is the delivery vehicle. The real signal comes from what the player actually notices, values, distrusts, or reveals.

### 4. Each card should create pressure, not demand a finished answer

A good card gives the scene a new problem, clue, object, admission, or tonal shift.

The interesting material often happens in the minute after the first addition, when the players stay in the moment and let the scene breathe.

### 5. Real disclosure works better when hidden inside fiction

The goal is not to force direct confession.

Better:
- a clue reveals what someone finds trustworthy
- a cover story leaks what someone is actually good at
- a suspicious object carries a real comfort or obsession

This preserves playfulness while still letting real information surface.

### 6. Structure helps, but over-instruction hurts

The deck should still have a shape:
- `Opening`
- `Random cards`
- `Midpoint`
- `More random cards`
- `Finale`

But individual cards should read more like in-world intelligence than like worksheets.

### 7. The fun is interpretive

Part of the experience is noticing what someone chooses to make important in the fiction.

Examples:
- what detail they notice first
- what they decide is suspicious
- what they treat as comforting
- what quality they make admirable
- what kind of explanation they improvise under pressure

## Recommended Play Loop

### House Rule

Stay in the scene, but steal from real life.

If a card asks for a detail, suspicion, instinct, preference, tell, or explanation, use your real one and let the story absorb it.

### Turn Rhythm

1. One player scans the card.
2. That player reads it silently.
3. That player immediately introduces the card's pressure in-world.
4. The other player responds in-world.
5. Stay with the moment for a bit.
6. Move on only when the scene feels spent.

### Response Rule

The non-scanning player does not solve the prompt. They just respond in the scene by doing one of these:
- accept it
- question it
- complicate it
- reinterpret it

### Tone Rule

Do not break character to explain what the card means unless you are actually stuck.

The phone should feel like private intelligence, not a game master.

## The Spyhouse

### Premise

You arrive separately at the same safehouse apartment under false names. You were both told to retrieve a package, trust no one, and leave quickly. But when you arrive, the package is already gone, the room has been disturbed in a very specific way, and it becomes clear that someone expected the two of you to meet.

### Suggested Structure

- Play `Opening` first.
- Shuffle the `12` random cards.
- Insert `Midpoint` after the sixth random card.
- End with `Finale`.
- For a shorter version, play `8` random cards total instead of all `12`.

### Opening

#### Opening / Dare-Reveal

You arrive at the safehouse apartment seconds apart, both under false names, both expecting to retrieve a package and leave. The package is gone. The place is mostly tidy, but something about the room says whoever left did so quickly and on purpose.

Say your alias, what you were told, and the first thing in the apartment that makes you think this night is going to get stranger.

Use the kind of first impression habit you actually have.

### Random Cards

#### 1. Something Is Off

One object in the apartment does not belong here.

Bring it into the scene. Choose the kind of detail you would notice immediately in a room like this.

#### 2. The Room Was Watching

You find a sign that whoever prepared this apartment expected one of you before the other.

Show the sign. Let it reveal the kind of thing you use to decide whether someone can be trusted.

#### 3. Locked Compartment

There is a drawer, case, or cabinet in the apartment that should have been empty but is not.

Open it. What you find should feel oddly personal for a professional setup, and it should connect to a real comfort, fascination, or small obsession of yours.

#### 4. Bad Cover Story

Your explanation for being here gets worse.

Say it anyway. Let the lie accidentally reveal something true about what you are actually good at.

#### 5. Someone Outside

You hear movement outside the door.

Pretend not to be worried. Show exactly how you try to look calm when you are not.

#### 6. Ridiculous Evidence

Something embarrassingly minor in the apartment suddenly becomes central evidence.

A receipt. A magnet. A candle. A record sleeve. Choose it, and make it matter far too much. Base it on a real pet peeve, tiny standard, or irrational preference you actually have.

#### 7. Unplanned Competence

You notice the other person doing something unexpectedly capable, careful, or kind.

Put that moment into the scene. Let it come from a real quality you genuinely find reassuring or attractive in people.

#### 8. The Assignment Was a Cover

It starts to feel as if the missing package mattered less than what this room was meant to reveal.

Let yourself say, or almost say, what you thought this assignment was really going to be.

#### 9. Too True

You say something that was meant to stay inside the cover identity, but came out a little too real.

Say it.

#### 10. The Strange Part

Begin with:

`The strange part was not the apartment. The strange part was...`

Finish the thought with the kind of vibe, behavior, or quality that genuinely catches your attention in people.

#### 11. Not My Real Name

Begin with:

`That wasn't my real name, but it was close to the version of me that...`

Finish it with a real side of yourself, smuggled through the alias.

#### 12. Why I Stayed

Somewhere in your next addition, include:

`I stayed because...`

Do not use the words `truth`, `lie`, `package`, or `name`.

What follows should come from a real instinct, pull, or preference you actually recognize in yourself.

### Midpoint

#### Midpoint / Dare-Reveal

A hidden message in the apartment makes one thing clear: one of your false names was expected, and the other was chosen as bait.

Reveal what form the message takes, and let it force out something you were trying not to show.

### Finale

#### Finale / Dare-Reveal

You finally understand the package was an excuse to get the two of you into the same room.

Decide who arranged it, what they were testing, and why neither of you left when you had the chance.

End on one image:
- a laugh
- an unopened door
- a shared plan
- the exchange of real names

## Notes For Seed Conversion

When converting this to `CardSight`:
- keep card text short enough to read privately on a phone
- prefer short headers plus concise body text
- avoid dual-player bullet choreography on random cards
- keep `Opening`, `Midpoint`, and `Finale` as the only explicit `Dare/Reveal` cards
- preserve the "real person inside fiction" language

## A Wonderous Heist

### Premise

You and the other person have entered the Museum of Impossible Things under false names. Somewhere inside is a vault holding something neither of you can define cleanly, but both of you believe you will recognize it when you see it.

The museum does not behave like an ordinary building. Rooms shift. Displays seem curated for the people who walk through them. Security responds less to trespassing than to motive.

The point of the story is not to invent a perfect caper. The point is to see what each of you chooses to admire, distrust, protect, or pursue once the museum starts reflecting you back at yourselves.

### Suggested Structure

- Play `Opening` first.
- Shuffle the `12` random cards.
- Insert `Midpoint` after the sixth random card.
- End with `Finale`.
- For a shorter version, play `8` random cards total instead of all `12`.

### Opening

#### Opening / Dare-Reveal

You enter the Museum of Impossible Things under false names, there to steal a single item from the Heart Vault before midnight.

Neither of you knows exactly what the target is yet, only that you are both certain you will recognize it when it appears.

Say your alias, what role you seem to play in the heist, and the first thing in the museum that makes you think this place was designed for people like you.

Let that first detail come from the kind of thing you would actually notice first in a strange room.

### Random Cards

#### 1. A Room That Should Not Exist

A new room appears that should not exist inside the museum.

Bring it into the scene. Make it the kind of place you would instantly want to inspect more closely.

#### 2. Trust Test

You find an instruction that may be helping you or may be studying you.

Bring it into the scene. Let it reveal one small thing that genuinely makes you trust someone faster, or one small thing that quietly puts you on guard.

#### 3. Wrong Motive

A security system activates, but it does not care whether you are armed or authorized. It cares why you came.

Show how your way through the room reveals something you would actually chase hard enough to embarrass yourself a little.

#### 4. Bad Cover Story

Your explanation for belonging in the museum gets worse.

Say it anyway. Let the lie accidentally reveal something true about what you are actually good at.

#### 5. Watched By A Guard

A guard sees you and is entirely unconvinced by whatever image you are trying to project.

Show the moment. Let the scene reveal how you try to seem smooth when you are improvising.

#### 6. Signature Move

In the middle of the heist, you do something completely unnecessary that somehow becomes part of your whole style.

Put it into the scene. Base it on one harmlessly ridiculous thing you are weirdly confident about.

#### 7. The Room Of Taken Things

You enter a quiet room full of objects other people once tried to steal because they mattered too much to leave behind.

Choose one object that stops you. Let it carry a real desire, comfort, or private wish of yours.

#### 8. Crisis Standard

The vault asks, without asking directly, what makes someone worth choosing when the pressure becomes real.

Answer in the scene. Let it come from a quality you genuinely find reassuring, attractive, or worth trusting.

#### 9. Unexpected Competence

You notice the other person doing something unexpectedly effective, careful, or kind during the heist.

Put that moment into the scene. Let it come from a real quality you admire in people.

#### 10. The Plan Almost Fell Apart

Begin with:

`The plan almost fell apart when I realized...`

Finish the thought with the kind of problem you would actually find most distracting, suspicious, or hard to ignore.

#### 11. The Kind Of Partner Who

Begin with:

`You were the kind of partner who...`

Finish it with a detail that works in the heist, but also hides a real compliment you would mean.

#### 12. The Room Opened For

Begin with:

`The room opened only for people who secretly valued...`

Finish it with a value you genuinely respect enough to notice in real life.

### Midpoint

#### Midpoint / Dare-Reveal

The Heart Vault sends a message:

`Warning: the target is not an object. Continue only if you are willing to be slightly more honest than your alias intended.`

Bring the moment into the scene. Let it force out what you think the vault may really contain, and let that guess borrow from something you actually hope for when a connection feels promising.

### Finale

#### Finale / Dare-Reveal

The Heart Vault opens.

Inside is not a jewel, file, or weapon. It is the thing this entire museum was arranged to uncover.

Decide what is inside, why you knew it mattered, and whether the right ending is to steal it, protect it, or share it.

End on the kind of ending you would actually find most satisfying for a night like this.
